A series in nine parts

Notes on the Deep Learning Tuning Playbook

Josh Speagle The Playbook on GitHub ↗

Google Research's Deep Learning Tuning Playbook is a rare thing in machine learning: a written account, by people who have trained neural networks for many years, of how they actually get good results in practice. Much of its advice fits in a sentence, such as re-tuning the learning rate whenever the batch size changes, or scoring a training run by its best checkpoint rather than its last. Why each of those sentences is true usually takes considerably longer to explain, and that explanation is what this series is about.

I wrote these posts to understand the Playbook myself. Coming to it as a statistician, I kept finding that the advice made the most sense once I could see the mechanism underneath it, so for each part we work through the argument, write down a simple model of what is going on, and build a small simulation wherever a picture helps us see why the advice holds. I'm sharing them in the hope that they are useful to others who are learning to tune models systematically, and to anyone curious about where these rules of thumb come from.

The Playbook and these notes

The Deep Learning Tuning Playbook was written by Varun Godbole, George E. Dahl, Justin Gilmer, Christopher J. Shallue, and Zachary Nado of Google Research (Brain Team) and Harvard University. It was released on GitHub in 2023 under a CC BY 4.0 license. It is written for engineers and researchers who want to get the best performance out of deep learning models, and it concentrates on the process of hyperparameter tuning for supervised problems. The authors present it as their opinions at the time of writing, and as a living document that will change as their beliefs change.

Each post covers one or more sections of the Playbook, though not in the Playbook's order. A few ideas, such as how a single training run should be scored, come up in almost every section, so I introduce them first and build on them later. Every post links to the sections it covers so that the two can be read side by side. The Playbook remains the primary reference, and these notes try to add the reasoning around it.

Where each kind of material comes from

  • The Playbook's advice

    Recommendations are attributed to the Playbook in the text and linked to the relevant section on GitHub. Where the Playbook has its own figure, you can open it from the toolbar of the corresponding interactive figure.

  • Mechanisms from the literature

    When an explanation comes from a published paper rather than the Playbook, it is cited where it appears and listed in the post's references.

  • Toy models

    The simulations are deliberately small, so that each one isolates a single mechanism. Their numbers follow from the assumptions listed under "How this toy works" in each figure, not from real training runs.

  • My own views

    Where I add my own judgment or go beyond what the Playbook says, I say so in the first person.

Who the posts are for

I have in mind a reader who has trained a few networks in PyTorch or JAX and knows what a learning rate, a validation set and an optimizer are, but who has not yet tuned a model systematically. The mathematics stays at the level of undergraduate calculus, linear algebra and introductory statistics, and each section keeps only one or two equations in the main text. Derivations and more technical asides live in collapsible boxes marked ƒ(x), which can be skipped without losing the thread.

Shaded terms show a short definition when you hover over or tap them. Most figures are interactive, and their toolbars offer a guided tour of what to look for, the Playbook's own version of the figure where one exists, and the assumptions behind the toy model.

The series

The nine parts fall into three acts. The first sets up how we run experiments we can trust, the second follows a single round of tuning from design to decision, and the third turns to training dynamics and the engineering that every round depends on. They read best in order, and each part links back to the earlier ideas it relies on.

Act I

Experimental method and setup

How to run experiments you can trust, and the decisions made once at the start of a project.

  1. Part 1 Tuning a network one question at a time The incremental tuning loop, scoring a run by its best checkpoint, and the roles that make comparisons fair.
  2. Part 2 Starting a new project Why to begin with a proven architecture, a popular optimizer and a deliberately simple first configuration.
  3. Part 3 Choosing the batch size The batch size sets training speed and cost, and the hardware and gradient noise set which sizes are worth using.
Act II

Running, reading and deciding

One round of experiments: placing the trials, checking what came back, and deciding what to keep.

  1. Part 4 Where to put your trials, and how many to run Grid, random and quasi-random search, when Bayesian optimization fits, and how many trials a study needs.
  2. Part 5 Reading the results of a tuning study Checking search-space boundaries and training curves before trusting the best trial, then comparing with isolation plots.
  3. Part 6 Deciding whether to adopt a change Seed-to-seed variation, re-running the best trial, and weighing a gain against the complexity it adds.
Act III

Dynamics, stability and engineering

How long to train, how to keep training stable, and the pipeline every round depends on.

  1. Part 7 How long to train Setting the number of training steps, with and without a compute limit, and adjusting the learning-rate schedule to match.
  2. Part 8 Finding and fixing training instabilities Recognizing an unstable run, and how learning-rate warmup and gradient clipping let a larger learning rate train.
  3. Part 9 Input pipelines, evaluation, checkpoints and multi-host training Input bottlenecks, periodic evaluation, checkpoints, experiment tracking, batch normalization and multi-host setups.
