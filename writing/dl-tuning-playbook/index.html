Notes on the Deep Learning Tuning Playbook

Josh Speagle The Playbook on GitHub ↗

Google Research's Deep Learning Tuning Playbook is a written account, by people who have trained neural networks for many years, of how they get good results in practice. Much of its advice fits in a sentence, such as re-tuning the learning rate when the batch size changes or scoring a run by its best checkpoint. The reasons behind each sentence take longer to explain.

I wrote these posts to understand the Playbook myself. For each part I worked through the argument, sketched the mechanism, and built small simulations where a picture helped me see why the advice holds. I am sharing them in the hope that they help other people who are learning to tune models systematically.

The Playbook and these notes

The Deep Learning Tuning Playbook was written by Varun Godbole, George E. Dahl, Justin Gilmer, Christopher J. Shallue, and Zachary Nado of Google Research (Brain Team) and Harvard University. It was released on GitHub in 2023 under a CC BY 4.0 license. It is written for engineers and researchers who want to get the best performance out of deep learning models, and it concentrates on the process of hyperparameter tuning for supervised problems. The authors present it as their opinions at the time of writing, and as a living document that will change as their beliefs change.

Each post covers one or more sections of the Playbook. The order differs from the Playbook's, because ideas that several sections rely on (how a single training run is scored, for example) come first. Every post links to the sections it covers, so you can read the two side by side. The Playbook remains the reference, and these notes add explanation around it.

Where each kind of material comes from

  • The Playbook's advice

    The posts attribute it in plain words ("The Playbook recommends...") and link to the section on GitHub. Figures reproduced from the Playbook open from a figure's toolbar, under "The Playbook's figure".

  • Mechanisms from the literature

    Explanations taken from published papers are cited where they appear and listed in each post's references.

  • Toy models

    I built small simulations to show one mechanism at a time. Their numbers follow from the assumptions listed under "How this toy works" in each figure, and do not come from real training runs.

  • My own views

    Opinions of mine are written in the first person, which keeps them separate from what the Playbook says.

Who the posts are for

The posts assume you have trained a few networks in PyTorch or JAX and know what a learning rate, a validation set and an optimizer are, but have not yet tuned a model systematically. The mathematics stays at the level of undergraduate calculus, linear algebra and introductory statistics. Each section keeps one or two equations in the main text. Derivations and more technical framing sit in collapsible boxes marked ƒ(x), which you can skip without losing the argument.

Shaded terms show a short definition when you hover over or tap them. Many figures are interactive. Their toolbars can open a guided tour of what to look for, the Playbook's own figure where one exists, and the assumptions behind the toy model.

The series

The nine parts are grouped in three acts. They read best in order, and each one links back to the earlier ideas it uses.

Act I

Experimental method and setup

How to run experiments you can trust, and the decisions made once at the start of a project.

  1. Part 1 Tuning a network one question at a time The incremental tuning loop, scoring a run by its best checkpoint, and the roles that make comparisons fair.
  2. Part 2 Starting a new project Why to begin with a proven architecture, a popular optimizer and a deliberately simple first configuration.
  3. Part 3 Choosing the batch size The batch size sets training speed and cost, and the hardware and gradient noise set which sizes are worth using.
Act II

Running, reading and deciding

One round of experiments: placing the trials, checking what came back, and deciding what to keep.

  1. Part 4 Where to put your trials, and how many to run Grid, random and quasi-random search, when Bayesian optimization fits, and how many trials a study needs.
  2. Part 5 Reading the results of a tuning study Checking search-space boundaries and training curves before trusting the best trial, then comparing with isolation plots.
  3. Part 6 Deciding whether to adopt a change Seed-to-seed variation, re-running the best trial, and weighing a gain against the complexity it adds.
Act III

Dynamics, stability and engineering

How long to train, how to keep training stable, and the pipeline every round depends on.

  1. Part 7 How long to train Setting the number of training steps, with and without a compute limit, and adjusting the learning-rate schedule to match.
  2. Part 8 Finding and fixing training instabilities Recognizing an unstable run, and how learning-rate warmup and gradient clipping let a larger learning rate train.
  3. Part 9 Input pipelines, evaluation, checkpoints and multi-host training Input bottlenecks, periodic evaluation, checkpoints, experiment tracking, batch normalization and multi-host setups.

The tuning process on one page

The Playbook describes tuning as a loop of rounds, each with one goal, preceded by a few decisions made once at the start. The flowchart lays out that process and marks which part of the series covers each step. The same chart closes every post, with the steps of that post marked.
