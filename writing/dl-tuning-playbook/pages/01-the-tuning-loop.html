Tuning a network one question at a time

Josh Speagle Notes on the Playbook's section on a scientific approach to improving model performance ↗

Part of a series of notes on Google Research's Deep Learning Tuning Playbook. Read what the Playbook is and how these notes work.

Suppose you have a network that trains and a validation score you want to improve. The tempting next move is to change several settings at once, or hand every choice in the config to an automated search overnight. Either move can lower the score, but neither explains why, and that missing insight makes every later decision harder. The Playbook argues instead for a slower loop where each round asks one question and is designed so its answer can be trusted. In this first post, I work through the four pieces that make that loop function: scoring one run, reading a set of runs together, comparing options fairly, and budgeting trials.

In brief
  • Ask one question per round: Change one isolated group of settings around a concrete goal instead of scrambling everything together (One question per round).
  • Score by the best checkpoint: Evaluate periodically and keep the lowest validation error on a consistent schedule across compared runs (Scoring one training run).
  • Tune nuisance parameters separately: Search learning rate and related optimizer settings independently for each candidate so defaults never skew the ranking (Making a comparison fair).
  • Balance range against density: Split your trial budget over a window wide enough to catch each optimum without starving the valley floor (Spending a fixed budget).

One question per round

Take a concrete situation. Validation accuracy has stalled a few points short of your target, and ideas pile up: switch optimizers, add dropout, add a learning-rate decay schedule, or deepen the network. If you try all four in one run and gain half a percent, you learn little. One change might drive the entire gain while another quietly hurts accuracy and slows training down.

The opposite strategy hands every hyperparameter to an automated search and lets the algorithm sort things out. Godbole and colleagues consider that impractical because the configuration space is so large. In their words, there are not yet "any algorithms sophisticated enough to efficiently search this space without human guidance." Even a coarse grid with three values across twelve hyperparameters has $3^{12} = 531{,}441$ points, so 50 or 100 runs barely sample it.

What the Playbook proposes instead is an incremental tuning strategy (the Playbook's section ↗). You begin from a simple configuration that already works and improve it over a sequence of rounds, each asking one narrow question. Within a round you run a study, the Playbook's term for a set of training runs at different hyperparameter settings, where each run is a trial. When the evidence says a change helps, you adopt it into your baseline configuration; the Playbook calls that update a launch whether or not anything ships.

The Playbook's summary · The incremental tuning strategy ↗

Start with a simple configuration and incrementally make improvements while building up insight into the problem. Make sure that any improvement is based on strong evidence to avoid adding unnecessary complexity.

Each round cycles through the four steps in Figure 1, and the first decides whether the other three can succeed. The Playbook asks for a goal narrow enough that one round can make real progress on it. Examples range from testing one potential improvement (a regularizer or preprocessing step), to mapping one hyperparameter such as an activation function, to greedily lowering validation error late in a project (choosing the goal ↗).

That last example shows how rounds are distributed over a project. According to the Playbook, most time goes to gaining insight, which it calls exploration, while comparatively little goes to greedily lowering validation error, or exploitation (exploration vs exploitation ↗). A round where a new regularizer fails to help still pays off if the plots show why. That insight tells you which settings move validation error, which interact and must be re-tuned together, and which can stay fixed. Only once the search space is mapped should the final stage switch to exploitation, which the Playbook suggests running with Bayesian optimization (Part 6).

Every step of the loop compares training runs, so first you must decide which single number represents a run.

Scoring one training run

Suppose you train for 20,000 steps with a hyperparameter configuration $\mathbf{h}$ bundling the learning rate, weight decay, and every other setting. To compare runs, you have to reduce each one to a single validation score, which the Playbook calls the trial's result. The natural default is the validation error at the final step, yet the end of training is often not where a run did best.

  • The run may have peaked earlier. A run with a high learning rate or little regularization can reach its best validation error partway through and then get worse as it starts to overfit the training set.
  • Validation measurements fluctuate. Even after the validation curve levels off, consecutive evaluations still move up and down. A single reading at the last step can hit a high point purely by chance.

Both problems share one remedy. The Playbook recommends running periodic evaluations at regular intervals, keeping the few best checkpoints on disk in a small buffer, and choosing the best of them when training ends (saving checkpoints ↗). In Playbook terminology this is retrospective optimal checkpoint selection, defining the trial's result as the minimum validation error across evaluation steps:

$$\hat{L}_{\text{trial}}(\mathbf{h}) \;=\; \min_{t \,\in\, \mathcal{T}_{\text{eval}}} L_{\text{val}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t).$$

Here $\boldsymbol{\theta}_t$ are the model weights at step $t$, $L_{\text{val}}$ is the validation error, and $\mathcal{T}_{\text{eval}}$ is the set of evaluation steps (every 1,000 steps in Figure 2). Because every trial runs to its full step budget and you pick the checkpoint afterwards, you never need an early-stopping rule guessing mid-run when to quit. That is why the Playbook considers prospective early stopping usually unnecessary.

In Figure 2 the run has stopped improving by around step 5,000, so the whole 0.0090 gap between the last checkpoint and the best one is evaluation noise. On a run that overfits, the gap would also include the damage done by the later training steps.

Why the best checkpoint reads below the plateau

The toy's plateau is exactly 0.2, yet the selected checkpoint reads 0.1899. Taking the minimum of 20 noisy evaluations picks out the largest downward fluctuation, so the minimum sits below the true plateau on average. Evaluating more often creates more chances for an unusually low reading. Over 300 seeds of the toy, the median gap between the last and selected checkpoint grows from 0.007 when evaluated every 2,500 steps to 0.011 at every 250 steps.

Here I go a step further than the Playbook, which does not discuss this bias. In my view it leaves two practical rules: evaluate every compared trial on the same schedule so none gets extra draws, and report final metrics on a separate test set because the best validation checkpoint is biased low, a small winner's curse.

Each trial now gives one number, but a round runs many trials, so next you need to read them together.

Reading a set of runs

Designing a study means choosing which hyperparameters vary across trials, the range each may take (together, the search space), and how many trials to run. A search algorithm then chooses each trial's settings within that space (creating a set of studies ↗). Figure 3 shows a study of 20 trials varying learning rate $\eta$ and weight decay $\lambda$ together. Each trial trains for 20,000 steps and reports its best checkpoint, and the run with the lowest result is the best trial.

A sorted table tells you which trial won, not whether the search space stood in the right place. For that question the Playbook recommends a basic hyperparameter axis plot, plotting each trial's validation score against one hyperparameter (identifying bad search space boundaries ↗). Look first at where the best trials sit: if they crowd one edge of the range, better values may lie outside, so widen the search in that direction and rerun.

In Figure 3 the best trial sits comfortably inside the learning-rate range, which is what you hope to see. Switching panel (b) to weight decay gives a less tidy picture. Points spread vertically because each trial used a different learning rate, so a good weight decay still scores badly when its learning rate is far off. On a projection like this, watch the lower edge of the cloud, which traces the best score reached at each weight decay.

That vertical spread hints at the next problem: a hyperparameter you are not studying can still decide how good an option looks.

Making a comparison fair

Take a concrete round. Your current model has four hidden layers, and you want to test whether six reduce validation error. The quickest experiment keeps everything else fixed, including the learning rate tuned for four layers, swaps the depth to six, and runs once.

The trouble is that the best learning rate generally depends on the architecture, so the value that suited four layers need not suit six. Running the six-layer model at the four-layer learning rate tests the deeper model at a setting tuned for the shallower one, and Figure 4 shows how that can mislead you.

The fix is to search the learning rate separately for each depth and compare the best result each depth reaches. The Playbook builds its entire approach to experimental design around this idea, assigning every hyperparameter in a round one of three roles (identifying scientific, nuisance and fixed hyperparameters ↗):

  • Scientific hyperparameters are the ones whose effect you want to measure; in this example, the number of hidden layers.
  • Nuisance hyperparameters are tuned separately for each value of the scientific hyperparameters so the comparison stays fair; here, the learning rate. You do not care about their values for their own sake, borrowing the statistical term for a nuisance parameter.
  • Fixed hyperparameters keep one value across the whole round. Fixing a hyperparameter saves trials at the cost of a caveat, because the conclusion may not hold at other settings.

With those roles defined, a round compares the best score each setting of the scientific hyperparameter reaches once the nuisance hyperparameters have been tuned:

$$L^\star(h_{\text{sci}}) \;=\; \min_{\mathbf{h}_{\text{nuis}}} \, L(h_{\text{sci}},\, \mathbf{h}_{\text{nuis}}).$$

Here $h_{\text{sci}}$ is one value of the scientific hyperparameter (say, six layers), $\mathbf{h}_{\text{nuis}}$ collects the nuisance hyperparameters, $L$ is the validation error, and the minimum runs over the nuisance search space. Godbole et al. call this operation "optimizing away" the nuisance hyperparameters. In practice you take the minimum over the trials you ran, so $L^\star$ is an estimate whose accuracy depends on how you spend those trials.

How much a fixed learning rate distorts the comparison

Suppose each option's validation error is a parabola in $x = \log_{10}\eta$ centered on its own best learning rate $x^\star(h_{\text{sci}})$, as in Figure 4:

$$L(h_{\text{sci}}, x) = L^\star(h_{\text{sci}}) + c\,\big(x - x^\star(h_{\text{sci}})\big)^2.$$

At a fixed learning rate $x_0$, option A scores $L^\star(A) + c\,(x_0 - x^\star_A)^2$ and option B scores $L^\star(B) + c\,(x_0 - x^\star_B)^2$, so holding $x_0$ fixed charges each model a penalty quadratic in its distance from $x_0$. Fixing $x_0$ at A's optimum leaves A unpenalized while B pays $c\,(x^\star_A - x^\star_B)^2$. In the toy that penalty is $0.05 \times 0.8^2 = 0.032$, larger than the true $0.015$ advantage, reversing the ranking.

Mathematically, $L^\star(h_{\text{sci}}) = \min_{\mathbf{h}_{\text{nuis}}} L(h_{\text{sci}}, \mathbf{h}_{\text{nuis}})$ takes the lower envelope over the nuisance parameters at each setting of interest. The Playbook does not name this construction beyond "optimizing away" nuisance hyperparameters, and that minimization is what keeps comparisons fair.

Which role does a hyperparameter take?

The Playbook stresses an important subtlety: roles belong to the round's goal, not to the hyperparameter itself. The activation function is scientific when comparing ReLU against tanh, nuisance when tuned separately for each depth, and fixed in an unrelated study on a ReLU network. Still, common hyperparameters fall predictably into each role:

  • Optimizer hyperparameters, such as learning rate, momentum, and Adam's $\beta$ parameters, are usually nuisance hyperparameters because they interact strongly with other changes. They are rarely scientific, since today's best learning rate rarely survives the next pipeline edit. Fixing some is acceptable when resources are tight or evidence shows they do not interact with the question at hand.
  • The choice of optimizer is usually scientific when comparing optimizers is the question, and fixed otherwise.
  • Regularization splits in two. Choosing whether to apply a method like dropout is scientific or fixed, while its strength parameter is a nuisance hyperparameter that remains nuisance across later rounds once adopted.
  • Architecture choices such as depth are usually scientific or fixed because, as the Playbook notes, they strongly change training speed and memory use.

Figure 5 applies these rules to three goals, using the Playbook's own examples. Pick a goal and press a hyperparameter to read why it takes its role, or switch to "Test yourself" and try assigning the roles first.

The third goal in Figure 5 raises one more complication, because each optimizer brings its own settings. Nesterov momentum adds a momentum coefficient, while Adam adds $\beta_1$, $\beta_2$ and $\epsilon$. Hyperparameters like these, which exist only for some values of the scientific hyperparameters, are what the Playbook calls conditional hyperparameters. Both optimizers do have a learning rate, yet the authors warn against sharing one search box, because the good ranges often differ by orders of magnitude. Give each optimizer its own learning-rate study.

Every nuisance hyperparameter you tune consumes trials, and compute sets the total trial budget in a round.

Spending a fixed budget of trials

How should a fixed number of trials be divided up? The authors frame this as a balance among three goals that any study design has to meet within a limited budget (striking a balance ↗):

  1. comparing enough different values of the scientific hyperparameters,
  2. tuning the nuisance hyperparameters over a large enough search space,
  3. sampling that search space densely enough.

Doing better on any one goal costs either more trials or progress on another. Suppose you can afford $K_{\text{total}} = 48$ trials to compare $M = 3$ scientific values, running one study per value. Each study gets $K$ trials across a learning-rate window $W$ decades wide (each decade is a factor of 10, so $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-1}$ spans three decades), giving a trial density

$$K = \frac{K_{\text{total}}}{M}, \qquad \rho = \frac{K}{W}.$$

With 16 trials over 3 decades, each study samples roughly 5.3 trials per decade. Whether that suffices depends on how the search range lines up with each option's learning-rate valley. Figure 6 illustrates what happens when the window is too narrow or too wide.

The two failure modes pull in opposite directions. A narrow range misses the valley floor completely, which happens easily because changing the scientific hyperparameter often shifts the best learning rate outside a window centered on the baseline. A wide range avoids that miss but spreads the trials thinly, so the closest point still sits high above the floor. Either way a strong option looks worse than it is, and the wrong candidate can win.

How far above the floor a sparse study's best trial sits

If $K$ trials are spread roughly evenly over $W$ decades, neighboring trials are about $W/K$ apart, so the trial nearest the best learning rate can be up to half of that away. Near its floor the curve is a parabola with curvature $c$, which means the best trial can sit as much as

$$\Delta L_{\max} \approx c\left(\frac{W}{2K}\right)^{2}$$

above the floor. In the toy of Figure 7, $c = 2.4$ per squared decade and $K = 16$. At $W = 1.375$ decades the bound is 0.004, while at $W = 5$ decades it is 0.059, ten times the 0.006 margin between the best option and the runner-up. Because the excess shrinks with the square of the density, doubling the trials per decade cuts it by a factor of four.

To see how often those failures flip the answer, Figure 7 repeats a full design many times. The toy tests three options for a scientific hyperparameter, each with its own learning-rate valley, where option 2 is best by six times the trial noise. For each range width, the figure reruns the design 200 times and tracks how often option 2 wins.

With 48 trials and three options, the design never picks the right option below 1.2 decades, because option 2's best learning rate lies outside the range. It picks correctly 98% of the time at 1.375 decades, but only 52% of the time at 5 decades, where the trials have thinned out. Adding a fourth option cuts each study to 12 trials and lowers the peak to 81%.

In a real round, of course, you never know where each option's optimum sits beforehand. The Playbook's practical recipe starts with a generous range and inspects the axis plots afterward, widening any range whose best trials crowd a boundary (Part 5).

The same budget arithmetic limits how many nuisance hyperparameters you can afford to tune. For Adam, the Playbook's FAQ offers a heuristic keyed to trial budget (how to tune Adam ↗): tune learning rate alone below 10 trials, add $\beta_1$ from 10 to 25, add $\epsilon$ from 25 upward, and tune $\beta_2$ once you can afford well past 25. Because the authors stress that general trial thresholds are hard to guarantee, I treat that ladder as a starting point rather than a rule.

In practice

Pulling these threads together, I find it helpful to answer six questions, in order, before launching a round. They condense the Playbook's section on designing the next round of experiments ↗, with each example tracking the depth comparison from Making a comparison fair.

  1. What is the one question?

    Write the goal as a single comparison that one plot could settle.

    ExampleDo six hidden layers give lower validation error than four?

  2. Which hyperparameter answers it?

    Mark it scientific, and keep its candidate list short.

    ExampleNumber of hidden layers, with values 4 and 6.

  3. What could shift when it changes?

    Mark interacting settings nuisance, starting with learning rate and optimizer settings.

    ExampleLearning rate and weight decay, searched separately for each depth.

  4. What will stay fixed?

    Mark unvaried choices fixed, and log the caveat each one adds.

    ExampleThe optimizer, dropout, and ReLU stay fixed, so the conclusion holds for this baseline.

  5. How will you spend the trials?

    Split the budget across candidates, span a generous learning-rate range, and cap nuisance parameters to what your trial budget supports.

    Example48 trials total, 24 per depth, searching learning rate over 3 decades.

  6. How will you score and check?

    Score each run by its best checkpoint on a fixed evaluation schedule, then check axis plots for crowded edges before reading the result.

    ExampleIf the six-layer best learning rate sits against the bottom edge, extend the range downward and rerun.

That leaves the last step of the loop: deciding whether to adopt the change. Making that decision reliably requires measuring run-to-run variability, the topic of Part 6.
