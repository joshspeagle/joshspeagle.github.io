How long to train

Josh Speagle Notes on the Playbook's section on the number of training steps ↗

Part of a series of notes on Google Research's Deep Learning Tuning Playbook. Read what the Playbook is and how these notes work.

Before launching a study, you must choose how many steps each trial trains for, and that choice shapes every comparison. Stop too early and models stay undertrained; train too long and dozens of trials waste compute on flat curves. The Playbook anchors that decision on whether extra compute would keep lowering validation loss. Below I separate those two regimes, derive the fixed-budget rules, examine learning-rate schedules including constant-then-decay cooldowns, and lay out tuning in rounds of increasing length.

In brief
  • Separate compute-bound from saturated workloads: Ask first whether curves are still improving at the end of a run (Two kinds of workload).
  • Fix max_train_steps inside every study: Calibrate an initial budget with a fast constant-rate sweep, then move it from best-checkpoint steps (One budget per study).
  • Match decay schedules to the horizon: Non-constant schedules boost late convergence, while trapezoidal cooldowns save pre-decay checkpoints (Learning-rate schedules).
  • Scale length in rounds when compute-bound: Run cheap Round-1 exploration, pair width transfer with horizon checks, and stretch high-rate steps in Round 2 (Tuning in rounds).

Two kinds of workload

Before choosing a step count, the Playbook sorts workloads into two regimes. Training is compute-bound when progress is limited by runtime or budget rather than dataset size $N_{\text{train}}$ or regularization. In that regime, more compute keeps lowering training loss and, when well tuned, validation loss too. Training is not compute-bound when you can afford longer runs, yet improvement stops or flips to overfitting well before the budget ends.

Existing training curves from early baselines usually classify the regime (Figure 1). Ask whether candidate runs are still improving right at their final steps. If so, treat the job as compute-bound, where more steps or an adjusted schedule can pay off. If validation and training scores flatten long before the final step, the run is not compute-bound and a shorter step budget suffices.

I find one implication of the compute-bound case clarifying. When time bounds optimization, speeding up step throughput or convergence produces the exact same outcome as extra runtime, making the effective target as long a run as you can afford. That does not mean raw runtime is the sole lever, a point the Playbook underscores.

One force crosses both regimes cleanly. Pipeline changes that increase gradient variance from step to step slow optimization progress and force larger step counts for equal validation error. The Playbook names three common culprits:

  • a smaller batch size,
  • adding data augmentation,
  • adding stochastic regularization such as dropout.

The two regimes pose distinct engineering problems. Without a compute bottleneck, you want enough steps for convergence without wasted flops; when compute-bound, runtime is capped, so you tune inside fixed step budgets. Following the Playbook, I work through saturated horizons first before turning to rounds for compute-bound runs.

Why every trial in a study gets the same budget

When training is not compute-bound, the target is enough steps for every reasonable configuration to peak without waste. When uncertain, the Playbook recommends erring slightly high. Under frequent checkpoints and retrospective checkpoint selection, adding steps preserves every earlier checkpoint candidate so the peak cannot worsen.

The Playbook on training that is not compute-bound ↗

Never tune the max_train_steps number in a study. Pick a value and use it for all trials. From these trials, plot the training step that retrospective checkpoint selection finds in order to refine the choice of max_train_steps.

The rule arrives without derivation, yet its logic follows directly from how decay schedules index progress. Standard schedules scale step size by elapsed fraction:

$$\eta_t = \eta \, s\!\left(\frac{t}{S_{\max}}\right),$$

where $\eta$ is the base learning rate, $S_{\max}$ is max_train_steps, and $s$ falls from 1 toward 0 (Figure 4 draws four common forms). Varying $S_{\max}$ inside a study gives two runs with identical $\eta$ different learning rates at every step plus different update totals. Holding $S_{\max}$ fixed isolates your scientific variables cleanly while still logging each trial's best step to adjust $S_{\max}$ in the next round.

A first value from a learning-rate sweep

That leaves a bootstrap problem for the opening study before any best checkpoints exist. The Playbook estimates initial steps from a constant-rate sweep trained until the model "perfectly fits" the training set. Here "perfect fit" is deliberately pragmatic, such as training misclassification reaching zero when cross-entropy keeps drifting down in unregularized logit growth.

The Playbook places this routine inside a collapsible box titled "Algorithm for picking an initial candidate for max_train_steps using a learning rate sweep" ↗, assuming the training split can be memorized at constant rate:

  1. Find any configuration that fits the training set. Its max_train_steps becomes a generous ceiling, $N$.
  2. Run a constant learning-rate sweep. Grid-search the learning rate with no data augmentation and no regularization, and train every trial for $N$ steps.
  3. Take the fastest trial's step count. The number of steps the fastest trial needs to reach perfect training performance is the initial guess for max_train_steps.
  4. Check the edges of the grid. If every rate tested is too slow, the estimated max_train_steps inflates sharply. Always verify the winning learning rate does not press against an edge of the sweep.

Why disable augmentation and regularization here? The Playbook does not spell out the reason, though in my reading both add step-to-step variance or cap memorization and would fold generalization drag into an optimizer speed test. Once you turn noise back on, you scale max_train_steps upward separately (see when to revisit the budget).

The boundary check matters more than it first appears because an edge-clipped sweep returns deceptive numbers that look healthy on loss curves. Figure 2 runs that failure mode in a small simulated problem with memorization set at training loss below 10⁻³.

At first glance the default window in Figure 2 reports 45,200 steps with clean training curves. Its only red flag is that the fastest trial hugs the upper grid boundary. Shifting the grid up one decade brackets the true minimum and cuts the initial budget nearly fourfold to 11,700 steps. Boundary checks apply across every sweep (Part 5), yet matter most here because every later study inherits this starting budget.

Reading where the best checkpoints fall

Once a fixed-budget study completes, inspect each trial's winning step $t_i^\star$ chosen by retrospective selection. Plotting them as normalized fractions of the budget keeps studies on one scale:

$$\rho_i = \frac{t_i^\star}{S_{\max}}, \qquad 0 \le \rho_i \le 1,$$

where $\rho_i$ marks when trial $i$ peaked (Figure 3). The Playbook gives two diagnostics:

  • Always in the first 10% ($\rho_i \le 0.1$ for every trial). "If the best step is always during the first 10% of training, then the maximum number of steps is way too high."
  • Consistently in the last 25% ($\rho_i > 0.75$). "If the best step is consistently in the last 25% of training we might benefit from training longer and re-tuning the decay schedule."

Those two thresholds are asymmetric for good reason. Peaking across every trial in the opening tenth leaves 90% of compute unused, proving clear over-budgeting. Late checkpoints carry weaker certainty because learning-rate decay drives error down near the finish even under adequate budgets, so the second rule pairs longer runs with re-tuning the schedule.

When to revisit the budget

A calibrated budget reflects the full training recipe rather than the model alone. Revisit max_train_steps whenever the pipeline changes in either direction:

  • More gradient noise, more steps. Raise the budget when steps grow noisier, for instance after adding data augmentation or dropout.
  • Better training, fewer steps. Lower max_train_steps when better optimizers or tuned schedules speed up convergence.

Batch size belongs in the first group because smaller batches raise gradient noise and step requirements, the scaling curve examined in Part 3. Check the normalized checkpoint fraction $\rho_i$ after any structural edit.

Learning-rate schedules

Step budgets and learning-rate schedules interact tightly, so you have to design them together. The Playbook recommends opening a project with a constant learning rate and adding decay once the baseline runs cleanly. Generous step budgets cushion tuning errors, whereas tight budgets require a carefully tuned decay schedule to reach strong validation scores.

Which family should you choose? In their FAQ ↗, Godbole et al. treat the optimal schedule family as an open question because isolating schedule geometry across different horizon lengths is notoriously difficult to benchmark cleanly. What holds reliably is that adding decay outperforms keeping a constant rate, and tuning its parameters matters. As defaults, the authors pick linear decay or cosine annealing [5], drawn alongside constant and inverse-square-root schedules in Figure 4.

Warmup, shown by the gray strip opening each panel of Figure 4, guards against early training divergence rather than convergence rate, so I leave it for Part 8.

The Playbook's FAQ also diagnoses where jagged hand-drawn schedules in papers come from. Often practitioners run a simple curve until validation accuracy plateaus, drop the step size manually, and repeat before a deadline. Copying that finished trajectory fails because its corners reflect specific model and batch settings; automated validation-reactive schedules are fine, whereas brittle manual restarts are best avoided.

Since the Playbook, horizon-free training has matured along two paths. Defazio et al. (2024) [1] show that Schedule-Free optimization replaces an explicit end-time $S_{\max}$ with continuous iterate averaging, matching tuned cosine schedules without freezing step budgets up front. Even better established in large pretraining is replacing end-to-end cosine decay with warmup-stable-decay cooldowns, examined alongside Round 2 extension below.

Tuning in rounds when training is compute-bound

Now consider the compute-bound regime. Loss keeps falling toward zero, so runtime and compute supply the only ceiling. Should every sweep run at full production length? The Playbook answers "not necessarily," trading relevance against search breadth (Figure 5). Shorter trials buy far more experiments early on while saving full-length compute for final candidates, though insights won at 10% length do not automatically survive scaling to 20% or 100%.

To balance both, the Playbook recommends tuning in rounds of increasing length. One to three rounds cover most projects cleanly; two rounds make an effective default:

  • Round 1: short runs screening model architectures and optimizer settings.
  • Round 2: a few full-length runs refining winning settings into the final model.

Multi-round tuning bets that configurations winning early also win at full length. The Playbook emphasizes there is no theorem behind this, ranking hyperparameter classes strictly as educated suspicions (Figure 6) while noting "we need more research."

The schedule sits last for good reason. Citing Wu et al. (2018) [6], the Playbook warns against choosing rates myopically over truncated horizons. Short runs inject short-horizon bias when gradient noise coexists with ill-conditioned curvature: tuning on small step counts favors overly timid learning rates. On a two-layer MNIST model, decay exponents tuned at 100 steps versus 20,000 steps diverged by over two orders of magnitude.

Why short runs favor small learning rates, in one dimension

Take one direction of the loss with curvature $\mu$, so $L(\theta) = \tfrac{\mu}{2}\theta^2$, and give each gradient independent noise $z_t$ with variance $\sigma_g^2$. One step of gradient descent with learning rate $\eta$ is

$$\theta_{t+1} = (1 - \eta\mu)\,\theta_t - \eta\, z_t.$$

The mean of $\theta$ shrinks by $(1 - \eta\mu)$ per step, so progress spans roughly $1/(\eta\mu)$ steps. Spread settles where shrinkage balances injected noise:

$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\theta^2\right]_\infty = \frac{\eta\,\sigma_g^2}{\mu\,(2 - \eta\mu)},$$

giving excess loss near $\eta\sigma_g^2/4$ for small $\eta\mu$. Lowering $\eta$ cuts this noise floor immediately along steep directions within a few steps, while slowing flat directions whose timescale $1/(\eta\mu)$ grows. Short runs capture the immediate drop before long flat tails register, biasing greedy tuning toward small $\eta$.

Keep Wu et al.'s scope clear: they analyze online meta-gradient updates rather than fixed random sweeps, so exact bias magnitudes differ. Still, their comparisons show constant-rate intermediate checkpoints favoring smaller rates than runs that finish a short cooldown. Comparing Round-1 trials after each completes its decay prevents truncating mid-run noise floors.

Separating width scaling from step horizon

Short-run rounds compress duration while keeping full model width fixed, whereas modern large-model pipelines often pair that axis with width scaling. Under Maximal Update Parametrization ($\mu$P) [7], tuning base learning rates and initialization scales on narrow proxy widths transfers cleanly at fixed token horizon, separating model size tuning from Round-2 duration scaling.

Extending the schedule for Round 2

Because schedules rarely carry over intact, moving from Round 1 to Round 2 requires reshaping how step size spends its budget. The Playbook warns against spending added steps at tiny rates, which happens automatically if you stretch absolute-step schedules like inverse square root (Figure 4) or resume an already decayed checkpoint.

The Playbook on Round 2, marked as speculation ↗

Run the best hyperparameter configuration from Round 1. (Speculation) Use the extra steps to extend the period of training at a high learning rate. E.g. if linear schedule then keep the length of the decay fixed from Round 1 and extend the period of constant lr in the beginning. For cosine decay, just keep the base lr from Round 1 and extend max_train_steps as in Chinchilla paper.

Notice that extending the schedule diverges by shape: linear decay freezes its final decay length while elongating the flat plateau, whereas cosine stretches across the entire run. Figure 7 contrasts both rules against the pitfall of resuming a finished run at rate zero.

Dragging Round 2 longer pushes the pitfall's low-rate tail toward 100%, because every added step runs after decay finishes. Linear extension shrinks its low-rate fraction while holding decay span fixed, whereas stretched cosine stays fixed at 50%. Notice, however, that share below $\eta/2$ only flags stalled tails and must be weighed against cooldown slope and shape (Figure 7).

The Playbook cites Chinchilla [3] here and notes doubt over whether schedules transfer across run lengths. Instead of re-testing tuned Round-1 decay parameters on new lengths, Hoffmann et al. matched cosine duration strictly to each target run, confirming that overly long cycles degrade performance. That finding supports tuning cycle horizon per budget rather than copying schedules across horizons.

Trapezoidal schedules and reusable checkpoints

Post-2023 language-model pretraining tested the Playbook's constant-then-decay hypothesis directly and offered a practical solution to its Round-2 dilemma. Hägele et al. (2024) [4] show across decoder models from 33M to 8B parameters that holding a constant rate followed by a short final cooldown matches tuned cosine decay, especially with a $1 - \sqrt{u}$ decay shape over the last 10–20% of steps.

Under that warmup-stable-decay structure, you no longer restart from step zero when extending compute: save checkpoints just before cooldown begins and launch longer continuations directly off the stable plateau. Two caveats separate their results from the Playbook's speculation. First, effective cooldowns scale loosely with total length (down to 10–20% or a sufficient step count) rather than keeping Round-1's decay length strictly constant. Second, constant-phase rates sit near half the optimal cosine peak, so changing schedule family requires re-tuning the base learning rate.

Finally, you are not locked into two rounds. Mature pipelines with huge production runs sometimes sequence exponentially growing horizons across three or more rounds, using each step scale to prune risky ideas cheaply, though two rounds suffice for most applied tuning.

In practice

Choose your horizon workflow by whether extra compute keeps lowering validation loss, distinguishing Playbook steps from post-2023 practice.

  1. Classify the workload from existing curves

    If validation error flattens well before step limits, treat training as not compute-bound; if curves still fall cleanly at the end, budget tuning in rounds (Two kinds of workload).

  2. Hold one max_train_steps fixed inside every study

    Never tune step budget alongside other hyperparameters inside a sweep; pick one horizon for all trials, erring high when uncertain (Why budgets stay fixed).

  3. Seed the first budget from a constant-rate sweep

    Turn off augmentation and regularization, record steps for the fastest rate to fit training data, and shift the grid if that rate hits an edge (Learning-rate sweep).

  4. Track best-checkpoint fractions across rounds

    If winning checkpoints always appear inside the first 10%, cut steps; if they cluster in the final 25%, raise steps and re-tune the decay, revisiting budgets when noise rises (Best checkpoint fractions).

  5. Use short Round-1 sweeps when compute-bound

    Run 10% length for architecture and optimizer checks (or narrow $\mu$P proxies for width transfer), comparing trials after each finishes a short cooldown (Tuning in rounds).

  6. Spend Round-2 growth at full learning rate

    Extend cosine horizons with the run, stretch trapezoidal plateaus while branching from pre-cooldown checkpoints (or consider Schedule-Free when horizon is open-ended), and never resume decayed tails at near-zero step size (Extending Round 2).
