How long to train

Josh Speagle Notes on the Playbook's section on the number of training steps ↗

Part of a series of notes on Google Research's Deep Learning Tuning Playbook. Read what the Playbook is and how these notes work.

Before a study can start we have to decide how many steps each trial will train for, and that single number shapes every comparison the study makes. If it is too small, we end up comparing configurations that never finished learning; if it is too large, a study of fifty trials spends most of its compute on runs that stopped improving long before the end. The Playbook's advice hinges on one question about the workload: if we could afford to train much longer, would the model keep getting better? I'll start from that question, then work through how to pick and refine a fixed step budget when the answer is no, why the learning-rate schedule is tangled up with that budget, and how to tune in rounds of increasing length when the answer is yes.

Two kinds of workload

Before saying anything about step counts, the Playbook sorts workloads into two kinds. Training is compute-bound when what limits it is how long we are willing to wait, rather than how much training data $N_{\text{train}}$ we have or some other factor. In that regime, training longer or more efficiently should keep lowering the training loss and, with proper tuning, the validation loss as well. Training is not compute-bound when we can afford to train as long as we like, but at some point training longer stops helping much (or starts to overfit).

Usually we can tell which regime we are in from training curves we already have. The Playbook's diagnostic is to ask whether the trials are still improving at the end of training: if they are, the workload is compute-bound and might benefit from more steps or a different learning-rate schedule. The complementary question is whether performance on the training and validation sets saturated long before the final step, in which case the workload is not compute-bound and we may be able to train for fewer steps.

I find one consequence of the compute-bound case clarifying. If training is limited by time, then making training faster is the same thing as making it better, and the "optimal" training time is simply as long as we can afford. That does not mean longer or faster training is the only route to better results, a point the Playbook is careful to add.

One effect cuts across both regimes. Changes that increase the variance of the gradients from batch to batch usually slow training down, which means they can raise the number of steps needed to reach a given validation loss. The Playbook's examples of such changes are:

  • a smaller batch size,
  • adding data augmentation,
  • adding some types of regularization, such as dropout.

The two regimes pose different problems. When training is not compute-bound, we need a step budget that is long enough without being wasteful; when it is compute-bound, the budget is fixed by what we can afford, and the problem becomes how to tune well inside it. Following the Playbook, we'll take them one at a time, starting with the first.

Why every trial in a study gets the same budget

When training is not compute-bound, the goal is to train long enough for the model to reach the best result it can, without wasting steps. When in doubt, the Playbook tells us to err on the side of training longer, and the reason is reassuring: as long as we save checkpoints often enough and score each run by its best one (retrospective checkpoint selection), a longer run should never score worse, because the best checkpoint of the shorter run is still among its candidates.

The Playbook on training that is not compute-bound ↗

Never tune the max_train_steps number in a study. Pick a value and use it for all trials. From these trials, plot the training step that retrospective checkpoint selection finds in order to refine the choice of max_train_steps.

The rule comes without a derivation, but I think the reason becomes clear once we look at how decay schedules work. Most schedules set the learning rate at step $t$ from the fraction of the run that has elapsed:

$$\eta_t = \eta \, s\!\left(\frac{t}{S_{\max}}\right),$$

where $\eta$ is the base learning rate, $S_{\max}$ is max_train_steps, and $s$ is a shape that starts at 1 and falls toward 0 (Figure 4 draws the common shapes). Two trials with the same $\eta$ but different $S_{\max}$ therefore use different learning rates at every step, and the longer one gets more updates on top of that. If a study sampled $S_{\max}$ alongside the other hyperparameters, their effects would be confounded with the effect of extra compute. Holding $S_{\max}$ fixed keeps the comparison about the hyperparameters we actually care about, while the best steps the study records become our evidence for changing $S_{\max}$ before the next one.

A first value from a learning-rate sweep

This leaves a chicken-and-egg problem for the very first study, before any best steps exist. The Playbook's starting point is the number of steps needed to "perfectly fit" the training set with a constant learning rate. The authors are explicit that "perfectly fit" is informal and just means a very low training loss. With a log loss and no regularization, for example, the training loss can keep creeping down until it hits floating-point limits as the weights grow, so they would call the training set fit around the time the training misclassification error reaches zero.

The procedure itself is tucked into a collapsed block titled "Algorithm for picking an initial candidate for max_train_steps using a learning rate sweep" ↗, and it assumes we can fit the training set perfectly, and with a constant learning rate.

  1. Find any configuration that fits the training set. Its max_train_steps becomes a generous ceiling, $N$.
  2. Run a constant learning-rate sweep. Grid-search the learning rate with no data augmentation and no regularization, and train every trial for $N$ steps.
  3. Take the fastest trial's step count. The number of steps the fastest trial needs to reach perfect training performance is the initial guess for max_train_steps.
  4. Check the edges of the grid. If every learning rate in the sweep is too small, you could conclude that a very large max_train_steps is needed. At a minimum, check that the best learning rate is not at the boundary of the search space.

Why switch off augmentation and regularization? The Playbook doesn't say, but my reading is that both either add gradient noise or work against fitting the training set, so leaving them on would fold their slowdown into what is meant to be a measurement of the optimizer's speed. That slowdown is handled separately, by raising the budget when noise is added (see when to revisit the budget).

The last step deserves more attention than its brevity suggests, because the failure it guards against looks like a perfectly reasonable result. To see this, Figure 2 runs the sweep on a small simulated problem, with "fit" defined as a training loss below 10⁻³.

The first grid in Figure 2 gives an answer of 45,200 steps, and nothing about the runs that fit looks wrong. The only warning sign is that the winner is the highest rate we tried. Once we move the grid up a decade, it brackets the fastest rate and the estimate drops to 11,700 steps. This boundary check comes up in every search the Playbook describes (Part 5 covers search-space boundaries in general), but it matters especially here, because the number it protects is the one every later study starts from.

Reading where the best checkpoints fall

Once a study has run at a fixed budget, we have more than a score for each trial: we also have its best step $t_i^\star$, the step whose checkpoint retrospective selection kept. These steps are the diagnostic the Playbook asks us to plot, and it is convenient to put every trial on the same scale by dividing by the budget:

$$\rho_i = \frac{t_i^\star}{S_{\max}}, \qquad 0 \le \rho_i \le 1,$$

where $\rho_i$ is the fraction of the run that trial $i$ had completed at its best checkpoint. The Playbook gives two rules for reading them:

  • Always in the first 10% ($\rho_i \le 0.1$ for every trial). "If the best step is always during the first 10% of training, then the maximum number of steps is way too high."
  • Consistently in the last 25% ($\rho_i > 0.75$). "If the best step is consistently in the last 25% of training we might benefit from training longer and re-tuning the decay schedule."

Notice that the two rules are not symmetric, and I think the wording tells us why. If every trial's best step falls in the first tenth, then 90% of every run was spent on steps that did not help, which is unambiguous waste. A late best step is much weaker evidence, because with a decay schedule the loss often drops during the final decay whether or not the budget is adequate, so we should expect late best steps either way. That is presumably why the second rule only says "might benefit" and pairs training longer with re-tuning the decay schedule.

When to revisit the budget

A budget calibrated this way is a property of the whole pipeline rather than a constant, and the ideal number of steps can shift when the architecture or the data changes. The Playbook's more specific guidance points in both directions:

  • More gradient noise, more steps. The starting value may need to grow when the training procedure gets noisier, for example when we add data augmentation or a regularizer such as dropout.
  • Better training, fewer steps. It may be possible to shrink max_train_steps when training improves, for example with a better-tuned optimizer or learning-rate schedule.

Batch size falls under the first case: halving it raises the gradient noise and usually raises the number of steps needed, which is the regime Part 3 studies in detail. Whatever the change, the way to check the budget is the same plot of best steps after the next study.

Learning-rate schedules

We have already seen twice that the budget and the learning-rate schedule are hard to separate, so it is worth looking at schedules directly. The Playbook suggests starting a project with a constant learning rate and adding a decay schedule later, while warning that schedules interact particularly strongly with the training budget. A generous budget makes tuning easier, whereas a very stingy one "might require a learning rate decay schedule tuned to perfection in order to achieve a good error rate."

Which schedule should we use? Godbole et al. admit in their FAQ ↗ that the best decay family is an open problem, largely because it is not clear how to design rigorous experiments that would settle it. What they are confident about is that having some non-constant schedule matters, and that tuning it matters, because different learning rates work best at different stages of training. Their default preference is linear or cosine decay, though they expect many other families to work well too.

Warmup, the short ramp at the start of each panel in Figure 4, addresses a different problem (instability in the first few steps), so the Playbook groups it with training instabilities and I'll leave it to Part 8.

The FAQ also offers a plausible origin story for the elaborate piecewise schedules that sometimes appear in papers. Many of them come from tuning by hand against the validation set: someone starts a run with a simple schedule, waits until performance stagnates, pauses, resumes with a steeper decay or a smaller rate, and repeats until the deadline. Copying such a schedule is a bad idea, the authors argue, because the best schedule depends on many other hyperparameters. It would be better to copy the algorithm that produced it, but that is rarely possible when the algorithm was human judgment. Schedules that respond to validation error are fine when they are fully automated, whereas human-in-the-loop versions are brittle and hard to reproduce, so the Playbook recommends avoiding them.

Tuning in rounds when training is compute-bound

Now we turn to the other regime. In a compute-bound workload the training loss keeps improving, and the only limits are our patience and our compute. Should every tuning trial then train for as long as we can afford? The Playbook's answer is "not necessarily," and the reason is a familiar trade-off between relevance and sample size. Shorter experiments let us run more of them and save the production-length runs for the models we hope to launch. As trials approach the patience limit they become more relevant to the final model, but we can complete fewer of them. The authors expect that many questions can be answered with runs about 10% of the production length, while acknowledging the risk that conclusions at that length will not hold at 20%, let alone 100%.

Their way of balancing the two is to tune in rounds of increasing length. In principle we can run as many rounds as we like, though one to three are usually the most practical. The idea is to learn as much as possible from trials with a quick turnaround, then lengthen the runs and keep tuning, double-checking earlier conclusions as needed. As a starting point the Playbook recommends two rounds:

  • Round 1: shorter runs to find good model and optimizer hyperparameters.
  • Round 2: very few long runs on good hyperparameter points to get the final model.

The whole scheme rests on an assumption we should examine: that a hyperparameter which wins in a short run still wins in a long one. The Playbook is candid that there is no guarantee. For some kinds of hyperparameters the two are often correlated enough for Round 1 to be useful, and the authors rank their suspicions by how likely each kind is to transfer, while noting that "we need more research."

The schedule sits at the bottom of the ranking, and here the Playbook points to Wu et al. (2018) on "the dangers of trying to pick learning rates myopically." Wu et al. called the effect short-horizon bias: when the learning rate or its decay is chosen by how well a run does after a small number of steps, the choice is biased toward step sizes that are too small. They show that the bias requires two ingredients, noisy gradients and a loss surface whose curvature differs a lot between directions, and most network training has both. The size of the effect can be large. In one of their experiments, a two-layer network on MNIST, the decay exponent tuned for a 100-step horizon and the one tuned for a 20,000-step horizon differed by more than two orders of magnitude.

Why short runs favor small learning rates, in one dimension

Take one direction of the loss with curvature $\mu$, so $L(\theta) = \tfrac{\mu}{2}\theta^2$, and give each gradient independent noise $z_t$ with variance $\sigma_g^2$. One step of gradient descent with learning rate $\eta$ is

$$\theta_{t+1} = (1 - \eta\mu)\,\theta_t - \eta\, z_t.$$

The mean of $\theta$ shrinks by the factor $(1 - \eta\mu)$ each step, so this direction needs on the order of $1/(\eta\mu)$ steps to make progress. The spread settles where the shrinkage balances the injected noise:

$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\theta^2\right]_\infty = \frac{\eta\,\sigma_g^2}{\mu\,(2 - \eta\mu)},$$

which gives an excess loss of about $\eta\sigma_g^2/4$ when $\eta\mu$ is small. Lowering $\eta$ therefore lowers this noise floor, and a high-curvature direction reaches its new, lower floor within a few steps. In a low-curvature direction, though, the same change slows the approach, since $1/(\eta\mu)$ grows. A short run sees the quick gain and stops before the slow cost appears, which is exactly the bias. Wu et al. analyze a many-dimensional version of this noisy quadratic and show that without noise, or with equal curvature in every direction, the greedy choice is optimal.

We should be careful about how far to carry this. Wu et al. study gradient-based tuning of the learning rate on small networks, which is quite different from the random searches the Playbook uses, so their results tell us the direction of the bias and its mechanism but not how large it would be in a Round 1 study. One of their figures does suggest a practical precaution: runs compared at a constant rate partway through favored small learning rates, while the same runs compared after a short decay favored larger ones. My reading is that Round 1 trials are best compared after each has finished its own decay, which the fixed-budget rule from earlier in this post already ensures.

Extending the schedule for Round 2

If the schedule is the least likely thing to transfer, then the biggest question in moving from one round to the next is what to do with it, and the Playbook names one common pitfall: "using all the extra training steps with too small of a learning rate." This happens whenever Round 2 keeps Round 1's schedule in absolute steps. A schedule with a time constant measured in steps, such as the inverse square root in Figure 4, falls into it by construction, and so does resuming Round 1's final checkpoint and continuing at its final learning rate.

The Playbook on Round 2, marked as speculation ↗

Run the best hyperparameter configuration from Round 1. (Speculation) Use the extra steps to extend the period of training at a high learning rate. E.g. if linear schedule then keep the length of the decay fixed from Round 1 and extend the period of constant lr in the beginning. For cosine decay, just keep the base lr from Round 1 and extend max_train_steps as in Chinchilla paper.

So extending the schedule means different things for the two default families. For a linear schedule it means a constant phase at the base rate followed by a decay of fixed length, while for cosine decay it means stretching the whole cosine over the longer run. To see how much this matters, Figure 7 compares both against the pitfall (Round 1's schedule continued at its final rate) and measures each by the fraction of Round 2 spent below half the base rate.

As we drag Round 2 longer, the pitfall's share climbs toward 100%, because every added step runs at Round 1's final rate. The linear extension moves the other way, since its decay keeps a fixed length while the run grows around it. The stretched cosine sits in between and always spends half of Round 2 below half the base rate, because stretching preserves its shape.

The cosine advice leans on the Chinchilla paper , which the Playbook elsewhere also reads as suggesting that even decay schedules transfer, a claim the authors doubt. What Hoffmann et al. actually did was match each cosine cycle to the length of its run, and they found that cycles more than about 25% longer than the run clearly hurt. That supports matching the cosine length to the run length. They did not, however, tune a schedule at one length and test it at another, so the Playbook's doubt about transfer still stands.

Since the Playbook

More recent work on language-model pretraining has tested this constant-then-decay shape directly. Hägele et al. (2024) found that a constant rate followed by a cooldown over the final 10 to 20% of steps performed about as well as a well-tuned cosine schedule, and that a longer run could resume from a checkpoint saved before the cooldown instead of starting over. That supports the direction of the Playbook's speculation, although the two disagree on the length of the decay: the Playbook keeps Round 1's decay length in steps, while Hägele et al. keep the cooldown a fixed fraction of the run. For long runs they found that a smaller fraction can work, as long as the cooldown still spans enough steps. They also found the best constant rate to be about half the best cosine peak, which means a base rate tuned for one family does not carry over to another. Their experiments used decoder-only language models from 33M to 8B parameters.

Finally, nothing limits us to two rounds. More can make sense for teams with mature pipelines and very expensive production runs, though the Playbook notes they will often be overkill. In the limit where analysis time did not matter and compute efficiency were the overriding concern, the authors' ideal would be to increase run length exponentially over many rounds, with each round checking that earlier choices still hold and each new idea passing through progressively longer, de-risking experiments.

In practice

Before the first study
Pick one budget and hold it fixed.
  1. Fix one max_train_steps per study. Never tune it inside a study. When unsure, err on the side of training longer. Why ↓
  2. Set the first value from a constant learning-rate sweep. Turn off augmentation and regularization, take the steps the fastest rate needs to fit the training set, and move the grid if that rate is at an edge. Procedure ↓
After each study
Read the best steps, then adjust.
  1. Plot each trial's best step. Always in the first 10% means the budget is far too long. Consistently in the last 25% suggests training longer and re-tuning the decay. Rules ↓
  2. Revisit the budget when the pipeline changes. More gradient noise (a smaller batch, augmentation, dropout) may need more steps, and better-tuned training may need fewer. Details ↓
When compute-bound
Trials are still improving when the budget runs out.
  1. Tune in rounds of increasing length. Round 1 uses short runs, around 10% of production length, for model and optimizer hyperparameters. Round 2 runs very few configurations at full length. Rounds ↓
  2. Spend Round 2's extra steps at a high learning rate. For linear decay, keep the decay length and extend the constant phase. For cosine, keep the base rate and stretch the schedule. Extension ↓
