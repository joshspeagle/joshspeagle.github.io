Reading the results of a tuning study

Josh Speagle Notes on the Playbook's section on extracting insight from experimental results ↗

Part of a series of notes on Google Research's Deep Learning Tuning Playbook. Read what the Playbook is and how these notes work.

A tuning study has finished, and you have a table of fifty runs sorted by validation error. Copying the top row into your config file is tempting. The Playbook asks a few questions about the study first, because a search range that is too narrow, too few samples or unhealthy training runs can each point to the wrong winner. Each question has a plot that answers it, and I found that once I knew what to look for, most of these plots took a minute to read.

Questions to ask before reading the winner

Every round of experiments has a goal, such as finding out whether weight decay helps. Before weighing the evidence a study gives toward that goal, the Playbook asks a set of questions about the study itself. Its reasoning is that the right questions often turn up problems that need fixing first, and that skipping them can lead to the wrong conclusion (the Playbook's section on extracting insight). The questions are:

  • Is the search space large enough? If the best trials sit near an edge of a searched range, a better value may lie beyond it.
  • Have enough points been sampled? If few trials fall in the good region, the best of them is partly luck.
  • What fraction of trials is infeasible? An infeasible trial is one that diverges, reaches a very bad loss, or fails to run. Many of them waste budget, and sometimes they point to a bug.
  • Does the model show optimization issues? Loss spikes and divergence get their own treatment in Part 8.
  • What do the training curves of the best trials show? For example, do they show problematic overfitting?

If an answer reveals a problem, the Playbook recommends refining the study (a better search space, more trials, or some other fix) and running it again before drawing conclusions. Experiments are expensive, so the authors also suggest reading each study for what else it can teach, even when that is not the round's goal.

Three kinds of plot answer nearly all of these questions (). The Playbook recommends generating the first two automatically for every study, since a plot that takes effort to make is a plot people skip (its section on automating plots).

Is the search space large enough?

Suppose you searched learning rates between $10^{-5}$ and $5 \times 10^{-4}$. You chose that range before running anything, so nothing guarantees that the best learning rate lies inside it. The Playbook's check is the basic hyperparameter axis plot: validation error on the vertical axis, one hyperparameter on the horizontal axis (on a log scale for the learning rate), and one point per trial. Each point should be the best validation error the trial reached at any point during training, which Part 1 covers.

The Playbook calls a search space suspicious when its best point is close to the boundary. If the best points cluster toward one edge, it recommends expanding the range in that direction until the best observed point is no longer near the edge (its section on bad search-space boundaries). Its Figure 1 shows a bad and an acceptable range from a real study, and it opens from the toolbar of . The Playbook does not put a number on "close". marks the outer 10% of the log range at each end as an edge zone, a working threshold of my own.

A run of failed trials above one learning rate changes that reading. The Playbook notes that when all trials above some learning rate are infeasible, and the best trials sit at the edge of that region, the model may have a stability problem that keeps it from using higher learning rates. In , 21 of 50 trials diverge, the lowest at a learning rate of 6.1 × 10⁻⁴, and the best trials sit just below. None of them is in an edge zone, because the range itself is wide enough. Widening it further would add more crosses. The remedies (learning-rate warmup, gradient clipping and others) belong to Part 8 on training instabilities.

Have you sampled enough points?

A range can contain the best learning rate and still say little about it, if one or two trials fell nearby. The Playbook is candid that "it can be very difficult to know" whether a search space was sampled densely enough. Its working practice is to sample what you can afford, then calibrate your confidence by looking at axis plots repeatedly and judging how many points fall in the good region. If there are too few, it suggests running more trials or being less ambitious about the tuning goal (its section on sampling enough points).

The case in shows the problem. It searches the same wide range with 10 trials instead of 50, and 2 of them come within 0.03 of the best error, against 8 in the 50-trial study. The 0.03 tolerance is a site convention; any fixed tolerance makes the same point.

A rough count shows why the good region empties quickly as a study grows. If trials are spread evenly in log space, the share of them that falls inside the good region along one hyperparameter is about the width of that region divided by the width of the range. With several tuned hyperparameters the shares multiply. For a study of $K$ trials,

$$K_{\text{good}} \approx K \left(\frac{\Delta}{W}\right)^{D},$$

where $\Delta$ is the width of the good region and $W$ the width of the searched range (both in decades, assumed the same for every axis), and $D$ is the number of tuned hyperparameters. The estimate is my own and does not come from the Playbook. It assumes a box-shaped good region and even coverage.

A study of 50 trials searches ranges 4.0 decades wide around a good region 1.0 decade wide in 2 tuned hyperparameters. About 3.1 trials fall in the good region.

Infeasible trials take budget too. The Playbook advises that when a very large fraction of a study's trials is infeasible, you adjust the search space to avoid sampling them, which sometimes requires reparameterizing it, and it warns that many infeasible trials can also indicate a bug in the training code. In the widened study, 6 of 50 trials diverge, all above 3.7 × 10⁻², more than a decade above the best trials. Lowering the upper end to just below the first failure would spend those trials closer to the best region without bringing it near an edge.

What do the training curves show?

So far each trial has been one number, its best validation error. Two trials with the same number can have very different runs behind them. The Playbook says the authors always look at the training curves (training error and validation error plotted against training step) of at least the best few trials of every study, because the curves are an easy way to spot common failure modes and to decide what to do next (its section on examining the training curves). It lists the questions to ask of them. sketches the curve shape behind each one, with the likely cause and the Playbook's suggested response.

The Playbook adds a subtlety about overfitting that matters whenever you pick a best trial. When a study compares values of a scientific hyperparameter (the one whose effect you want to measure), check the best trial at each of those values for problematic overfitting. If any of them overfits, the Playbook recommends rerunning with more regularization before comparing. Its example is a study of the number of hidden layers. If the best run with the most layers overfits, try it again with more regularization instead of concluding that fewer layers are better. The exception is a study whose scientific hyperparameter is itself a regularizer, where weak settings are expected to overfit.

Picking the best trial also favors whatever happens to regularize, the Playbook points out. Anything that makes training worse can act as a regularizer, including a learning rate small enough to slow optimization down. So the best trial at each setting can be selected for a "bad" value of some other hyperparameter, and problematic overfitting in any trial of the study, best or not, is worth a look.

Step-to-step noise matters for a reason that is easy to miss. Each trial's score depends on which evaluation step happened to be lowest. Late noise makes that a matter of luck, which blurs the comparison between settings and makes the best trial hard to reproduce in production, where the final model may not stop on the same lucky step.

Did the change help? Isolation plots

Once a study passes these checks, you can read what it says about your goal. Suppose the goal is to find the best weight decay for your model. You try five weight-decay values. Each value needs its own learning rate, because the best learning rate can move when the weight decay changes. So you train each of the five values with 20 different learning rates, which makes 100 runs. This is the design behind Figure 2 of the Playbook.

Plotted against weight decay, the 100 runs form five columns, as in panel (a) of . The spread within a column mostly reflects the learning rate, good or bad. The question "how good can this weight decay be?" is answered by the best run in its column. Keep that one run per column, drop the rest, and you get panel (b).

The Playbook defines an isolation plot as a special case of the axis plot in which each point is the best trial across some or all of the nuisance hyperparameters. Nuisance hyperparameters are the ones you have to tune for a fair comparison but are not asking about, here the learning rate. In the Playbook's words, the plot shows performance after "optimizing away" the nuisance hyperparameters, which makes an apples-to-apples comparison between values of the scientific hyperparameter. For weight decay $\lambda$ and learning rate $\eta$, the point at each $\lambda$ is

$$\hat{L}_{\text{iso}}(\lambda) = \min_{k \in \mathcal{T}(\lambda)} \hat{L}_{\text{trial}}(\lambda, \eta_k),$$

where $\mathcal{T}(\lambda)$ is the set of trials run with weight decay $\lambda$, $\eta_k$ is the learning rate of trial $k$, and $\hat{L}_{\text{trial}}$ is a trial's best validation error.

Quasi-random search does not produce neat columns. Every trial has its own weight decay, so no two share a value. The Playbook's approximation is to cut the horizontal axis into buckets and take the best trial in each vertical slice. does this for a synthetic 100-trial study over learning rate and weight decay. Panel (a) shows where each trial sat in the two-dimensional search space. Panel (b) shows the same trials against weight decay alone, with the best trial of each bucket joined into the isolation curve.

The number of buckets is a trade-off that the Playbook leaves to the reader. Wide buckets hold many trials, so their best trial is close to the best that range of weight decay can do, but the curve has few points. Narrow buckets give more points, but some will hold no trial near a good learning rate and sit too high.

Why sparse buckets sit too high

Write $L^*(\lambda)$ for the error at weight decay $\lambda$ with a perfectly tuned learning rate. A bucket's best trial uses one of the few learning rates that happened to fall in the bucket, so without noise its error is at least $L^*(\lambda)$, and the gap shrinks as more trials fall in the bucket. With evaluation noise, the minimum of many noisy trials also tends to come out below its expected value, which pulls the other way. The second effect is the subject of Part 6.

Comparing against a baseline

Often the question is whether weight decay belongs in the model. The Playbook's answer is to compare the best point of the isolation plot against the baseline with no weight decay, with one condition: "For a fair comparison, the baseline should also have its learning rate equally well tuned."

The condition is easy to break without noticing. Suppose that in your model, adding weight decay moves the best learning rate from about 0.013 down to 0.0032. You tune the learning rate carefully for the weight-decay runs and settle on the range 0.0025 to 0.004. Then, to save compute, you restrict the baseline to those same rates. shows the result in a toy where both models are equally good once tuned.

In practice

I now follow this routine after every study, in the order the Playbook suggests. Each step names what to look for and what to do about it.

  1. Plot every tuned hyperparameter

    Best trials near one edge → expand the range that way and rerun.

    A run of failed trials just above the best trials → treat it as instability (Part 8).

  2. Count the good region and the failures

    One or two trials near the best → run more trials or narrow the goal.

    Many infeasible trials → move or reparameterize the range, and check for bugs.

  3. Open the curves of the best few trials

    Match them against . Fix overfitting, noise or a bug before comparing settings.

  4. Read the goal from an isolation plot

    Use the best trial at each value of the scientific hyperparameter. With quasi-random search, bucket the axis.

  5. Tune the baseline as well as the candidate

    Give it the same nuisance hyperparameters and search effort, and check that its best trial is not at an edge.

The Playbook closes its section on plots by paraphrasing Geoffrey Hinton: "Every time you plot something new, you learn something new." The authors automatically generate axis plots for every varied hyperparameter and training curves for every trial (its section on automating plots).
