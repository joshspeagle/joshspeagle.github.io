Reading the results of a tuning study

Josh Speagle Notes on the Playbook's section on extracting insight from experimental results ↗

Part of a series of notes on Google Research's Deep Learning Tuning Playbook. Read what the Playbook is and how these notes work.

Suppose a tuning study has just finished and we are looking at a table of fifty runs sorted by validation error. The natural thing to do is copy the top row into the config file and move on, but the top row is only as trustworthy as the study that produced it. A search range that was too narrow, too few samples in the region that matters, or training runs that went quietly wrong can each crown the wrong winner. The Playbook's response is to interrogate the study before believing its answer, and I have come to think of this as a short diagnostic pass in which each question has a plot that answers it, most of which take a minute to read once we know what to look for. We'll start with the edges of the search space and the density of sampling, look at what the training curves of the best runs reveal, and then turn to isolation plots, which is where the study finally answers its question, and to the easily broken requirement that the baseline be tuned as carefully as the candidate.

Questions to ask before reading the winner

Every round of experiments has a goal, such as finding out whether weight decay helps. It is tempting to go straight to that goal, but the evidence a study offers is only as good as the study itself, so the Playbook first asks a handful of questions about how the study went. The authors' reasoning is that these questions often turn up problems that need fixing before anything else, and that skipping them is an easy way to reach the wrong conclusion (the Playbook's section on extracting insight). There are five of them:

  • Is the search space large enough? If the best trials sit near an edge of a searched range, a better value may lie beyond it.
  • Have enough points been sampled? If few trials fall in the good region, the best of them is partly luck.
  • What fraction of trials is infeasible? An infeasible trial is one that diverges, reaches a very bad loss, or fails to run. Many of them waste budget, and sometimes they point to a bug.
  • Does the model show optimization issues? Loss spikes and divergence get their own treatment in Part 8.
  • What do the training curves of the best trials show? For example, do they show problematic overfitting?

When one of these answers reveals a problem, the recommended response is to refine the study (with a better search space, more trials, or whatever else the problem calls for) and run it again before drawing any conclusions. And because experiments are expensive, the authors also suggest mining each study for whatever else it can teach us, even when that lies outside the round's stated goal.

Conveniently, three kinds of plot answer nearly all of these questions, and sketches each of them. The Playbook recommends generating the first two automatically for every study, on the reasonable grounds that a plot which takes effort to make is a plot people skip (its section on automating plots).

Is the search space large enough?

The most basic way a study can mislead us is through its range. Suppose we searched learning rates between $10^{-5}$ and $5 \times 10^{-4}$. We chose that range before running anything, so nothing guarantees that the best learning rate actually lies inside it. The check the Playbook proposes is the basic hyperparameter axis plot, which puts validation error on the vertical axis and one hyperparameter on the horizontal axis (on a log scale, in the case of the learning rate), with one point per trial. Each point should be the best validation error that trial reached at any time during training, a choice we discussed in Part 1.

A search space is suspicious, in the Playbook's terms, when its best point lies close to the boundary. If the best points cluster toward one edge, the remedy is to expand the range in that direction until the best observed point is no longer near the edge (its section on bad search-space boundaries). The Playbook's own Figure 1 shows a bad and an acceptable range from a real study, and it can be opened from the toolbar of . Since the authors never put a number on "close", I needed a working threshold for the toy, so marks the outer 10% of the log range at each end as an edge zone.

In other words, the narrow study's "winner" was simply the largest learning rate it was allowed to try, which tells us about the range rather than about the model.

The same picture means something different when a run of failed trials begins above some learning rate. The Playbook points out that when every trial above a certain learning rate is infeasible and the best trials sit right at the edge of that region, the model may have a stability problem that prevents it from using higher learning rates. In , 21 of 50 trials diverge, the lowest at a learning rate of 6.1 × 10⁻⁴, and the best trials sit just below it. None of them falls in an edge zone, because the range itself is already wide enough, so widening it further would only add more crosses. What the study is telling us is that the limit lies in the training, not the search, and the remedies (learning-rate warmup, gradient clipping and others) belong to Part 8 on training instabilities.

Have we sampled enough points?

A range can contain the best learning rate and still tell us very little about it, if only one or two trials happened to land nearby. Here the Playbook is refreshingly candid: "it can be very difficult to know" whether a search space was sampled densely enough. The authors' working practice is to sample what we can afford and then calibrate our confidence by looking at axis plots repeatedly, judging how many points fall in the good region. If there are too few, the options are to run more trials or to be less ambitious about the tuning goal (its section on sampling enough points).

We can see the problem in the case of , which searches the same wide range with 10 trials instead of 50. Only 2 of them come within 0.03 of the best error, compared with 8 in the 50-trial study, so the bottom of the valley is described by almost nothing. (The 0.03 tolerance is simply a convenient cutoff for counting nearby runs.)

Why does the good region empty out so quickly? I find a rough count helpful here, especially once a study tunes more than one hyperparameter. If trials are spread evenly in log space, the fraction that lands inside the good region along one axis is roughly the width of that region divided by the width of the range, and with several tuned hyperparameters these fractions multiply. For a study of $K$ trials we therefore expect

$$K_{\text{good}} \approx K \left(\frac{\Delta}{W}\right)^{D},$$

where $\Delta$ is the width of the good region and $W$ the width of the searched range (both in decades, and assumed the same for every axis), and $D$ is the number of tuned hyperparameters. This back-of-the-envelope estimate is my own rather than the Playbook's and assumes a box-shaped good region with even coverage, but it captures why the count falls geometrically with $D$, so a budget that comfortably covers one hyperparameter can leave only a few trials in the good region once we tune several. Infeasible trials eat into the same budget. When a very large fraction of a study's trials is infeasible, the Playbook advises adjusting the search space to avoid sampling them, which sometimes means reparameterizing it, and it warns that a high failure rate can also point to a bug in the training code. In the widened study, 6 of 50 trials diverge, all above 3.7 × 10⁻², which is more than a decade above the best trials. Lowering the upper end to just below the first failure would spend those trials closer to the best region without pushing the best region toward an edge.

What do the training curves show?

So far we have reduced each trial to a single number, its best validation error, but two trials with the same number can have very different runs behind them. This is why the Playbook's authors always look at the training curves (training and validation error plotted against training step) of at least the best few trials in every study, because the curves are a cheap way to spot common failure modes and to decide what to do next (its section on examining the training curves). They pose a short list of questions to ask of the curves, and sketches the shape behind each one alongside its likely cause and the suggested response.

Overfitting deserves a closer look, because it interacts with how we pick a best trial. When a study compares values of a scientific hyperparameter (the one whose effect we want to measure), the Playbook asks us to check the best trial at each of those values for problematic overfitting, and to rerun with more regularization before comparing if any of them overfits. Its example is a study of the number of hidden layers, where if the best run with the most layers overfits, the right move is to try it again with more regularization rather than to conclude that fewer layers are better. The exception is a study whose scientific hyperparameter is itself a regularizer, since there we expect the weak settings to overfit.

There is a second, subtler effect. Because selecting the best trial favors whatever happens to regularize, and almost anything that makes training worse can act as a regularizer (including a learning rate small enough to slow optimization down), the best trial at each setting can end up selected for a "bad" value of some other hyperparameter. The Playbook's conclusion is that problematic overfitting in any trial of the study, best or not, is worth investigating.

Step-to-step noise matters for a reason that is easy to miss. Since each trial is scored by its lowest evaluation, noise late in training turns that score partly into a matter of luck. This blurs the comparison between settings, and it also makes the best trial hard to reproduce in production, where the final model may not stop on the same lucky step.

Did the change help? Reading an isolation plot

Once a study has passed these checks, we can finally ask what it says about our goal. Suppose that goal is to find the best weight decay for our model, and we try five weight-decay values. Each value needs its own learning rate, because the best learning rate can move when the weight decay changes, so we train each of the five values with 20 different learning rates, for 100 runs in total. This is the design behind Figure 2 of the Playbook.

Plotted against weight decay, the 100 runs form five columns, as in panel (a) of . Most of the spread within a column reflects whether the learning rate was good or bad, which is not what we are asking about. The question we care about is "how good can this weight decay be?", and the best run in its column answers it. If we keep that one run per column and drop the rest, we get panel (b).

This is what the Playbook calls an isolation plot, a special case of the axis plot in which each point is the best trial across some or all of the nuisance hyperparameters, meaning the ones we have to tune for a fair comparison but are not asking about (here, the learning rate). In the authors' phrase, the plot shows performance after "optimizing away" the nuisance hyperparameters, which keeps the comparison between values of the scientific hyperparameter on an equal footing. Statistically, I find it helpful to think of this as a profile. For weight decay $\lambda$ and learning rate $\eta$, the point at each $\lambda$ is

$$\hat{L}_{\text{iso}}(\lambda) = \min_{k \in \mathcal{T}(\lambda)} \hat{L}_{\text{trial}}(\lambda, \eta_k),$$

where $\mathcal{T}(\lambda)$ is the set of trials run with weight decay $\lambda$, $\eta_k$ is the learning rate of trial $k$, and $\hat{L}_{\text{trial}}$ is a trial's best validation error. Just as a profile likelihood maximizes over nuisance parameters at each value of the parameter of interest, the isolation plot minimizes over the nuisance hyperparameters at each value of the scientific one.

In practice, though, the quasi-random search from Part 4 does not produce neat columns, because every trial has its own weight decay and no two share a value. The Playbook's workaround is to cut the horizontal axis into buckets and take the best trial in each vertical slice. does this for a synthetic 100-trial study over learning rate and weight decay, where panel (a) shows where each trial sat in the two-dimensional search space, and panel (b) shows the same trials against weight decay alone, with the best trial of each bucket joined into the isolation curve.

How many buckets should we use? The Playbook leaves this to the reader, and it is a genuine trade-off between resolution and reliability. Wide buckets hold many trials, so each bucket's best is close to the best that range of weight decay can do, but the curve has only a few points. Narrow buckets give a finer curve, but some of them will hold no trial near a good learning rate and so will sit too high.

Why sparse buckets sit too high

Write $L^*(\lambda)$ for the error at weight decay $\lambda$ with a perfectly tuned learning rate. A bucket's best trial uses one of the few learning rates that happened to fall in the bucket, so without noise its error is at least $L^*(\lambda)$, and the gap shrinks as more trials fall in the bucket. Evaluation noise pulls the other way, because the minimum of many noisy trials tends to come out below its expected value (a version of the winner's curse). That second effect is the subject of Part 6.

Comparing against a baseline

Often the question is not which weight decay is best but whether weight decay belongs in the model at all. The Playbook's answer is to compare the best point of the isolation plot against the baseline with no weight decay, subject to one condition: "For a fair comparison, the baseline should also have its learning rate equally well tuned."

That condition sounds obvious, but it is easy to break without noticing. Suppose that in our model, adding weight decay moves the best learning rate from about 0.013 down to 0.0032. We tune the learning rate carefully for the weight-decay runs and settle on the range 0.0025 to 0.004, and then, to save compute, we restrict the baseline to those same rates. shows what happens in a toy where both models are equally good once tuned.

In practice

Putting the pieces together gives a routine we can run after every study, in roughly the order the Playbook suggests. Each step below names what to look for and what to do about it.

  1. Plot every tuned hyperparameter

    Best trials near one edge → expand the range that way and rerun.

    A run of failed trials just above the best trials → treat it as instability (Part 8).

  2. Count the good region and the failures

    One or two trials near the best → run more trials or narrow the goal.

    Many infeasible trials → move or reparameterize the range, and check for bugs.

  3. Open the curves of the best few trials

    Match them against . Fix overfitting, noise or a bug before comparing settings.

  4. Read the goal from an isolation plot

    Use the best trial at each value of the scientific hyperparameter. With quasi-random search, bucket the axis.

  5. Tune the baseline as well as the candidate

    Give it the same nuisance hyperparameters and search effort, and check that its best trial is not at an edge.

The Playbook closes its section on plots by paraphrasing Geoffrey Hinton: "Every time you plot something new, you learn something new." In that spirit, the authors automatically generate axis plots for every varied hyperparameter and training curves for every trial (its section on automating plots), so that looking costs nothing.

Where this fits

Reading a study sits between running it (Part 4) and deciding whether to adopt a change (Part 6). It is also where the loop most often doubles back, as a clipped range sends us back to design a new search space, while unstable curves send us on to Part 8.
