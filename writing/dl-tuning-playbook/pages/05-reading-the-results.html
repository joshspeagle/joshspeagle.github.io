Reading the results of a tuning study

Josh Speagle Notes on the Playbook's section on extracting insight from experimental results ↗

Part of a series of notes on Google Research's Deep Learning Tuning Playbook. Read what the Playbook is and how these notes work.

Suppose a tuning study finishes and hands you a table of fifty runs sorted by validation error. Copying the top row straight into your config feels natural, yet that winner is only as trustworthy as the study behind it. A narrow search window, sparse sampling near the floor, or broken training curves can crown the wrong configuration. The Playbook's safeguard is a fast visual audit before reading off any winner. Below I trace that workflow: checking axis plots for boundary clipping and density, scanning training curves for overfitting or bugs, isolating scientific parameters cleanly, and holding the baseline to the same standard.

In brief
  • Audit the study before trusting row one: Inspect search bounds, sample count in the good basin, infeasible rate, and training curves first (Questions before reading the winner).
  • Separate boundary clipping from instability: Best trials crowded against a clean edge mean widen the range; best trials pressed against diverged failures point to instability (Is the search space large enough?).
  • Read training curves and isolation plots together: Screen top runs for overfitting or cutoff, then plot the minimum over nuisance settings per scientific level (Reading an isolation plot).
  • Tune baseline and candidate with equal effort: Reusing candidate search bounds on the baseline manufactures phantom wins (Comparing against a baseline).

Questions to ask before reading the winner

Every round targets a specific question, such as whether weight decay helps. Jumping straight to the winning number risks misreading a flawed sweep, so the Playbook [1] poses five diagnostic checks first (extracting insight):

  • Is the search space large enough? If the best trials sit against one end of a searched range, better values may lie outside it.
  • Have enough points been sampled? When few trials fall near the optimum, whichever configuration happened to score best owes much to luck.
  • What fraction of trials is infeasible? An infeasible trial diverges, finishes with poor loss, or crashes, wasting budget and sometimes exposing a bug.
  • Does the model show optimization issues? Loss spikes and divergence receive dedicated treatment in Part 8.
  • What do the training curves of the best trials show? Do top runs show problematic overfitting or premature termination?

Whenever those checks reveal trouble, refine the study (widen bounds, add trials, or patch the pipeline) and rerun before drawing conclusions. Since experiments cost real compute, mine every completed study for extra insight even outside the current goal.

Three plot types answer almost all of those checks, as sketched in Figure 1. Generate the first two automatically after every study, because plots that take manual work are plots engineers skip (automating plots).

Is the search space large enough?

A narrow window is the easiest trap in tuning. Suppose you swept learning rates from $10^{-5}$ to $5 \times 10^{-4}$; picking that interval upfront gives no guarantee the true optimum falls inside it. Plot each trial's best validation score against one hyperparameter on a basic hyperparameter axis plot (log-scaled for learning rate, Part 1).

A search space fails this check when top trials crowd against one boundary. When they do, widen the range on that side until the best points move comfortably inside (bad boundaries). You can open the Playbook's Figure 1 directly from the toolbar of Figure 2. Because the Playbook sets no hard pixel cutoff for "near," I shade the outer 10% of the log window as a visual edge zone in the toy.

In the (the default), all 3 best trials sit in the upper edge zone with best error 0.275. Widening to centers the best trials and lowers error to 0.238, proving the narrow run's "winner" only reflected an artificial ceiling.

An abrupt wall of failed runs signals a different blocker. When every trial above some learning rate diverges and top feasible runs press right against that wall, instability is blocking higher step sizes. In , 21 of 50 runs diverge starting at 6.1 × 10⁻⁴. Widening further just spawns more failures; fixing the optimization block requires warmup, gradient clipping, or normalization fixes (Part 8 on training instabilities).

Have enough points been sampled?

A box can bracket the true optimum yet tell you little if only one or two points happen to fall nearby. The Playbook notes candidly that "it can be very difficult to know" when coverage suffices. The practical habit is inspecting axis plots routinely to count points in the good basin; if sampling is too sparse, add trials or shrink the number of simultaneously tuned knobs (sampling enough points).

The preset in Figure 2 drops the budget from 50 trials to 10 over the same wide span. Only 2 trials finish within 0.03 of the minimum versus 8 at 50 trials, barely sampling the valley floor. (That 0.03 error tolerance is my own working cutoff for "near the valley minimum," not a Playbook rule.)

Why does a good region empty out so fast in multi-parameter studies? Think in volumes in log space: along one tuned axis of width $W$, a good interval of width $\Delta$ catches roughly fraction $\Delta / W$, and across $D$ independent tuned dimensions those fractions multiply:

$$K_{\text{good}} \approx K \left(\frac{\Delta}{W}\right)^{D},$$

where $\Delta$ and $W$ are decades wide along each of the $D$ tuned hyperparameters. This back-of-the-envelope estimate is my own rule of thumb rather than the Playbook's, assuming an axis-aligned target region. In Figure 2's widened 50-trial study where validation depends primarily on learning rate ($D = 1$, modulo infeasible trials), 8 runs finish within 0.03 of best; once two parameters must hit 1-decade boxes across 4-decade spans ($D = 2$), expected hits drop to 3.1. Infeasible runs drain that budget further. When failures consume a large share of trials, the Playbook recommends shifting bounds, reparameterizing, or hunting for training bugs. In the widened study, 6 of 50 trials diverge above 3.7 × 10⁻², well past top trials; trimming the top ceiling reclaims those samples cleanly.

What do the training curves show?

Single checkpoint numbers discard how runs evolved. That is why the Playbook's authors routinely plot training curves (train and validation error versus step) for the best few runs of every study (training curves). Figure 3 illustrates the six diagnostic curve shapes and their fixes.

Overfitting interacts strongly with trial selection. When comparing values of a scientific hyperparameter, inspect top trials at each setting for overfitting; if a deeper model overfits, retest with stronger regularization before assuming smaller models win. The natural exception is varying a regularizer itself, where unregularized settings should overfit.

Selection hides another trap: because picking the lowest error rewards anything that dampens overfitting (even a sluggish learning rate), an unregularized study may crown a sub-optimal optimizer configuration just for slowing training down. Treat overfitting in any top trial as a red flag worth inspecting.

Step-to-step evaluation noise cuts both ways. Scoring runs by their minimum checkpoint turns noisy tails into luck, blurring subtle scientific differences and risking non-reproducible final checkpoints.

Did the change help? Reading an isolation plot

Once a study clears these checks, it can answer the scientific goal. Suppose you sweep five weight-decay values while retuning 20 learning rates at each setting (100 runs total, matching Playbook Figure 2).

Plotted against weight decay, those 100 runs stack into five vertical columns (Figure 4a). Within-column spread mostly reflects good versus bad learning rates; the bottom point in each column answers how well that weight decay performs once learning rate is tuned, yielding panel (b).

This is an isolation plot, keeping the lowest error across nuisance hyperparameters (here learning rate) at each scientific value. In Playbook terms, it traces performance after "optimizing away" nuisance knobs so comparisons stay fair:

$$\hat{L}_{\text{iso}}(\lambda) = \min_{k \in \mathcal{T}(\lambda)} \hat{L}_{\text{trial}}(\lambda, \eta_k),$$

where $\mathcal{T}(\lambda)$ collects trials at weight decay $\lambda$, $\eta_k$ is trial $k$'s learning rate, and $\hat{L}_{\text{trial}}$ is its best validation error. Taking the minimum over nuisance settings at each scientific value computes the lower envelope after nuisance optimization is removed.

Because quasi-random search assigns distinct weight decays to every run instead of neat columns, cut the horizontal axis into buckets and connect each slice's best trial (Figure 5).

How many buckets should you pick? The Playbook leaves that choice to you, trading curve smoothness against sample count per slice: wide buckets hold plenty of trials yet localize the best value coarsely, whereas overly thin slices miss good learning rates and jitter upward.

Why sparse buckets sit too high

Write $L^*(\lambda)$ for validation error at weight decay $\lambda$ under an exact optimal learning rate. Without noise, any finite bucket's winner sits at or above $L^*(\lambda)$, shrinking as more trials fall inside that slice. Evaluation noise pulls downward via winner's curse (Part 6).

Comparing against a baseline

Often you want to know whether weight decay belongs at all. The Playbook compares the best isolation point against the baseline without weight decay under one strict condition: "For a fair comparison, the baseline should also have its learning rate equally well tuned."

That rule breaks silently whenever reuse saves compute in the wrong place. Suppose weight decay shifts optimal learning rate from 0.013 down to 0.0032; locking the baseline to the candidate's narrowed range (0.0025 to 0.004) invents a fake win even when tuned models tie (Figure 6).

Panel (b)'s apparent gain comes strictly from where the baseline was evaluated. Applying the boundary check separately to each arm catches this at once: confirm each arm's best trial sits inside its own learning-rate window, rather than pressed against an edge as in Figure 2.

Once a study clears all checks and both arms are balanced, one final check remains: whether the measured improvement rises above run-to-run variance (Part 6).

In practice

Run this five-step inspection after every completed study, in roughly the Playbook's order, before trusting the winning configuration.

  1. Plot every tuned hyperparameter

    Best trials near one edge: widen the search range that way and rerun.
    Wall of failed runs past best trials: diagnose instability before widening further (Part 8).

  2. Count the good region and the failures

    Only one or two trials near the floor: add trials or trim dimensions.
    Many infeasible trials: shift or reparameterize bounds and inspect for bugs.

  3. Open the curves of the best few trials

    Compare them against Figure 3; resolve overfitting, excessive tail noise, or pipeline bugs before drawing conclusions.

  4. Read the goal from an isolation plot

    Compare the best trial at each scientific setting once nuisance parameters adjust, bucketing the horizontal axis for quasi-random runs.

  5. Tune the baseline as well as the candidate

    Give the baseline identical nuisance axes and budget, verifying neither arm crowds its search edge.

The Playbook closes its section on plots by paraphrasing Geoffrey Hinton: "Every time you plot something new, you learn something new." To make checking effortless, generate axis plots and training curves automatically for every sweep (automating plots).

Where this fits

Reading a study sits between running it (Part 4) and deciding whether to launch the change (Part 6). Whenever plots catch clipped bounds or instability, the loop doubles back cleanly instead of locking in a false result.
