Finding and fixing training instabilities

Josh Speagle Notes on the Playbook's section on optimization failures ↗

Part of a series of notes on Google Research's Deep Learning Tuning Playbook. Read what the Playbook is and how these notes work.

If we push the learning rate high enough, any network's training loss will eventually spike or turn into NaN. Most of the time that failure simply marks the top of the useful range and tells us nothing more. Occasionally, though, the best learning rate in a sweep sits right at the edge of the failures, which suggests the model would train faster and end up better with larger steps if only it could survive them. The Playbook's section on optimization failures is about recognizing that situation and widening the range of usable learning rates. I want to start from a one-parameter quadratic that shows why a step can be too large, use it to read the signature of an unstable sweep, and then look at the two main remedies, learning-rate warmup and gradient clipping, before collecting everything into a short practical recipe.

When an instability matters

Any learning-rate sweep over a wide enough range has a failing end, where trials diverge to NaN or their loss climbs and stays high. So when should a failure worry us? Not simply because it exists, as the Playbook puts it bluntly:

Any workload will become unstable if the learning rate is too large. Instability is only an issue when it forces us to use a learning rate that's too small.

Deep Learning Tuning Playbook, "Identifying unstable workloads" ↗

In this sense a training instability is really a problem with the learning rate's range. Larger learning rates usually make faster progress per step, so a model that would prefer a larger rate but cannot survive one trains more slowly and often ends up worse. Figure 1 sketches the distinction. In a healthy sweep the loss falls as the rate grows, reaches a minimum, and rises again before training fails outright. In an unstable workload the failures set in before that minimum is reached, which means the best rate we can measure is just the largest one that still trains, and the true optimum is hidden behind the failures.

It also matters when a run breaks, because the remedies differ. The Playbook separates two kinds of instability:

  • Early instability happens at initialization or in the first part of training. The loss spikes within the first few steps, and the run either diverges or recovers and then trains slowly.
  • Mid-training instability is a sudden blow-up in the middle of training, which the Playbook associates with outlier gradient norms.

Later work has identified a third pattern in large Transformers: a slow divergence toward the end of training driven by growing output logits. In those experiments an auxiliary loss on the logits (z-loss) resolved it, while weight decay mitigated it for the larger models . The Playbook's advice targets the first two types, and before turning to the fixes it helps to understand why a step can be too large in the first place.

Why a step can be too large

Why should a larger step ever make things worse, when it points downhill? The simplest place to see the answer is a one-dimensional quadratic. Let's take the loss $\ell(\theta) = \tfrac{1}{2} \mu \theta^2$, where $\theta$ is a single weight and $\mu$ is the curvature (how sharply the loss bends around its minimum). The gradient is $\mu\theta$, so one step of gradient descent with learning rate $\eta$ gives

$$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \mu \theta_t = (1 - \eta \mu)\,\theta_t .$$

Every step multiplies the distance to the minimum by the same factor, $1 - \eta \mu$, so everything depends on that factor's sign and size. When $\eta \mu < 1$ it is positive, and the weight approaches the minimum from one side. When $1 < \eta \mu < 2$ it is negative but smaller than one in magnitude, so the weight jumps to the other side of the minimum on every step while still ending a little closer each time. Once $\eta \mu > 2$ its magnitude exceeds one, and every step overshoots farther than the last. The largest stable learning rate for this loss is therefore $2/\mu$, which means the more sharply the loss bends, the smaller the step it can tolerate.

A real network's loss has millions of directions rather than one, and each has its own curvature. These curvatures are the eigenvalues of the loss's Hessian (the matrix of second derivatives), and the largest of them is called the sharpness, $\mu_{\max}$. Near a minimum the loss is close to a quadratic, so gradient descent acts on each eigen-direction separately and the most curved direction is the first to fail. On a quadratic, gradient descent therefore diverges once $\eta > 2/\mu_{\max}$ , and I will call $2/\mu_{\max}$ the stability limit.

Why the most curved direction sets the limit

Near a minimum $\theta^\star$, write the loss as $\ell(\theta) \approx \ell(\theta^\star) + \tfrac{1}{2}(\theta - \theta^\star)^\top H (\theta - \theta^\star)$ with Hessian $H$. Expand the offset in the eigenvectors $v_i$ of $H$, with eigenvalues $\mu_i$: $\theta - \theta^\star = \sum_i a_i v_i$. One gradient step maps $a_i \mapsto (1 - \eta\mu_i)\, a_i$, which is the one-dimensional update applied to each direction on its own. Every coefficient shrinks only if $|1 - \eta\mu_i| < 1$ for all $i$, which is $\eta < 2/\mu_{\max}$.

Momentum changes the constant 2 by a factor of order one , and for Adam there is no clean analogue (Gilmer et al. fit an empirical limit of about $40/\eta$ in their appendix) . So we should read $2/\mu_{\max}$ as setting the scale of the limit rather than its exact value.

What makes this more than a textbook exercise is that a real network's sharpness changes as it trains. Two results, progressive sharpening (Cohen et al.) and the flatter-region picture of Gilmer et al., account for most of the Playbook's advice in my view. Under full-batch gradient descent the sharpness tends to rise during training until it reaches about $2/\eta$, and then it hovers there while the loss keeps falling over the long run, with brief rises along the way . Cohen et al. call this regime the edge of stability, and one consequence is that small upward blips in the loss near the limit are normal rather than alarming. Gilmer et al. studied the opposite direction. Models that start out sharp can diverge even at small learning rates, and training succeeds once the parameters reach a region where the sharpness is below $2/\eta$ . A slowly rising learning rate pushes the parameters toward such flatter regions, and they measured the sharpness tracking about $2/\eta$ through much of warmup. They present this push as the mechanism behind warmup, and we will return to it with a toy model in the warmup section. First, though, we need a way to tell whether a given sweep is limited by an instability at all.

Identifying an unstable workload

The practical question, then, is whether the best learning rate in our latest sweep is the true optimum or merely the largest rate that survives an instability. Conveniently, the Playbook's answer starts from the sweep we already have. If the best trial sits right next to the infeasible ones (trials that produce NaNs or "uncharacteristically high values of the loss"), and the trials just above it show the loss going up during training, then the workload is unstable and fixing the instability will likely let us train better. Figure 3 lays out this procedure together with two supporting checks: evaluating every step early in training, and logging the gradient norm.

To see what this signature looks like, Figure 4 runs the procedure on a toy model. Panel (b) shows the training loss at the selected rate twice, once as an evaluation every 100 steps would record it and once as an every-step log records it. At $\text{lr}^*$ the loss spikes to 9.57 times its starting value at step 14 and recovers by step 100, so the coarser schedule never sees the spike at all. This is exactly the kind of transient that the Playbook's every-step check is designed to catch.

The rate at which the model first becomes unstable gets its own name in the Playbook, the unstable_base_learning_rate. Because it serves as the reference point for the warmup recipe below, it is worth pinning down between the best feasible grid point and the first diverging one, which is what the bracket in Figure 4 does.

What to try

Once a sweep shows that an instability is limiting the learning rate, the natural first reaction is to lower the learning rate until the spikes go away. The Playbook deliberately puts that option at the bottom of its list, as "a last resort", and the reasoning is sound: a lower rate slows down the entire run to avoid a problem that may last only a few hundred steps or a handful of outlier batches. Figure 5 collects the full list of potential fixes.

The architecture item is the vaguest of these, and related work helps fill it in. Goyal et al. initialized the last batch-normalization scale in each residual block to zero, so that each block starts out as the identity, and found that this eased optimization early in training with large batches . For Transformers, Wortsman et al. normalized the queries and keys in attention (qk-layernorm). Without it, the learning rate at which their models diverged fell as the models grew, while with it the models trained to low loss at high learning rates . Still, warmup and clipping are the Playbook's main tools, and since each comes with a concrete recipe, they are the subject of the next two sections.

Learning-rate warmup

If the trouble comes in the first few steps, when the model is still sharp, an obvious idea is to take small steps until it is not. That is what learning-rate warmup does: training starts at a small learning rate and ramps up to the base rate over the first $W$ steps (warmup_steps). With a linear ramp from zero, the rate at step $t$ is

$$\eta_t = \eta_{\text{base}} \cdot \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{t}{W}\right).$$

The Playbook recommends warmup when a sweep looks like Figure 4, with the best rate at the edge of the infeasible ones and the loss rising in the runs above it. Its recipe is worth noticing for its order of operations, because it fixes an ambitious target rate first and only then searches for the shortest ramp that reaches it.

One part of this recipe has since been qualified. Wortsman et al. (2023) found that warmups of up to 25% of training, well past the Playbook's 10% cap, still reduced the sensitivity of Transformers to the learning rate and lowered their loss.

We can watch the recipe at work by running the sweep of step 4 in the same toy as Figure 4, which is what Figure 7 does. The unstable base learning rate is $u =$ 0.0316, so the target is $10u \approx 0.316$, and with max_train_steps at 100,000 the ramp may be at most 10,000 steps long. As the recipe prescribes, each candidate runs for its ramp plus twice that length at the base rate. Panel (a) shows the schedule against the toy's stability limit, $2/\mu_{\max}$, where $\mu_{\max}$ is the toy's sharpness (the curvature of its sharp direction) at each step, and panel (b) keeps a record of each length you try.

Why a ramp works and a step does not

In the toy, the sharp direction's curvature at step 0 sets the limit at 0.02, sixteen times below the target rate of 0.316, so jumping straight to the target lands far past the limit. A ramp succeeds because the learning rate does two things at once: it sets the size of each step, and it also pushes the parameters toward flatter regions, which raises the limit. A slow ramp gives the sharpness time to fall ahead of the rate, whereas a fast one crosses the limit while the sharpness is still high, which is how the 100- and 300-step ramps in Figure 7 fail.

The "hold, then jump" schedule lets us pull those two effects apart. A constant rate pushes the sharpness down only until the limit sits just above that rate, which matches Gilmer et al.'s observation that the sharpness settles near $2/\eta$. Holding 0.0158 for 1,000 steps keeps training stable, but in the toy that rate is too low to start the push at all, so the limit stays at 0.02. The jump to 0.316 at step 1,000 then lands about sixteen times past the limit, and the run diverges at step 1,002. Even a longer or higher hold would raise the limit only to about the hold rate, still far below the target. Goyal et al. saw the same failure in real networks: a constant low-rate warmup followed by a jump made the training error spike, and on ResNet-50 with large batches it ended worse than no warmup at all (25.88% top-1 error against 24.84%), while a gradual ramp gave 23.74% . The Playbook's recipe already uses a ramp, and I read these results as a good reason to keep it that way.

I should stress that the toy is a deliberate simplification. A real network's sharpness follows no formula like the toy's target, and Gilmer et al. also report models that did not benefit from warmup at the learning rates they tried, as well as one Transformer without layer normalization that warmup alone could not make train .

Gradient clipping

Warmup addresses instability at the start of training, but it can do nothing about a run that blows up deep into training because of a few outlier batches. For that mid-training failure, which the Playbook ties to outlier gradient norms, the natural tool is gradient clipping, which simply caps the size of each update. Whenever the gradient norm $\lVert g \rVert$ exceeds a threshold $c$, the gradient is rescaled to have norm $c$:

$$g' = c \, \frac{g}{\lVert g \rVert} \qquad \text{when } \lVert g \rVert > c .$$

The update keeps its direction while its length shrinks to $c$. Everything then hinges on the choice of $c$, and the Playbook's answer is to set it from what the model's own gradients look like, with the ideal threshold "just above the 'typical' gradient norm".

The 50% rule in that recipe deserves a moment, because it is not obvious why clipping most updates should amount to a smaller learning rate. With plain SGD or momentum the update is proportional to the gradient, so clipping a step multiplies that step's learning rate by $c/\lVert g \rVert$. If nearly every step is clipped, the run is effectively training at a learning rate scaled down by the average of that factor. Gilmer et al. describe a concrete case: a model clipped at 1.0 while its gradient norms sat around 5.0 was, in their words, "artificially lowering the learning rate by a factor of 1/5", and they raised the threshold to 6.0 .

Figure 9 makes this quantitative using the Playbook's two default plots for a synthetic log of 5,000 unclipped gradient norms, a series with an early stretch of elevated norms and 25 isolated spikes. With the threshold at the 90th percentile, $c =$ 1.71, clipping acts on 10% of the steps, catching the spikes and most of the early stretch, while the average factor applied to the gradient (the mean multiplier) stays at 0.97. Lowering the threshold to the median norm, 1.04, clips half the steps and drops the mean multiplier to 0.88. At 0.40, 99% of steps are clipped and the mean multiplier is 0.40, which is close to cutting the learning rate by more than half.

Real gradient norms differ in the details. In the Playbook's Figure 9, whose plot title names an unstable ResNet-200, the early norms start around $10^5$ as far as I can read from the plot, and the figure's notes say clipping stabilized early training. The logic of the procedure carries over unchanged, though. We place the threshold just above where the norm usually sits, so that it acts only on the outliers, and we watch the fraction of clipped steps to tell us whether clipping has quietly turned into a learning-rate cut.
