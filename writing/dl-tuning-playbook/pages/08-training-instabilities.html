Finding and fixing training instabilities

Josh Speagle Notes on the Playbook's section on optimization failures ↗

Part of a series of notes on Google Research's Deep Learning Tuning Playbook. Read what the Playbook is and how these notes work.

Push the learning rate high enough and any network's training loss eventually spikes or turns into NaN. Usually that ceiling just marks the top of the useful window. Occasionally, however, a sweep's winning learning rate sits jammed against failing trials, signaling that the model could improve with larger steps if it survived them. Below I explain curvature limits for gradient and adaptive updates, show how to verify an unstable sweep, walk through warmup and gradient clipping, and finish with modern Transformer and low-precision safeguards.

In brief
  • Distinguish harmless ceilings from blocked sweeps: Widen fixes only when winning rates crowd failing runs with rising loss curves above (Identifying an unstable workload).
  • Trace step limits to loss curvature: Gradient descent overshoots past $2/\mu_{\max}$, while adaptive methods equilibrate the preconditioned Hessian (Why a step can be too large).
  • Use warmup for early transients: Ramp toward $10\times$ the unstable rate only as slowly as curvature relaxation demands (Learning-rate warmup).
  • Clip outliers and harden architectures: Cap gradient spikes near the 90th percentile alongside Pre-LN, RMSNorm, QK-Norm, and z-loss (Gradient clipping).

When an instability matters

Wide learning-rate sweeps always have a breaking point where trials turn to NaN or shoot upward and stall. Why treat that failure as a bug rather than a natural bound? The Playbook states the test plainly:

Any workload will become unstable if the learning rate is too large. Instability is only an issue when it forces us to use a learning rate that's too small.

Deep Learning Tuning Playbook, "Identifying unstable workloads" ↗

Seen that way, a training instability narrows your usable learning-rate range (Figure 1). Healthy sweeps bend cleanly through a minimum and rise well before trials diverge. Unstable runs diverge ahead of that valley floor, stranding your best trial against a cliff while hiding faster convergence behind broken steps.

When a run fails selects the remedy. The Playbook distinguishes two instability classes:

  • Early instability happens at initialization or in the first part of training. The loss spikes within the first few steps, and the run either diverges or recovers and then trains slowly.
  • Mid-training instability is a sudden blow-up in the middle of training, which the Playbook associates with outlier gradient norms.

Post-2023 Transformer runs also highlight a third, slower divergence later in training from unbounded logit growth, discussed alongside architectural defenses in Stabilizing large transformers and low precision. The Playbook targets the first two patterns; before examining its fixes, Figure 2 shows why a downhill gradient step can diverge.

Why a step can be too large

How can a step pointing straight downhill raise the loss? A one-dimensional quadratic captures the exact geometry. Take $\ell(\theta) = \tfrac{1}{2} \mu \theta^2$, where $\theta$ is one parameter and $\mu$ is its curvature. Since the gradient is $\mu\theta$, one gradient step at rate $\eta$ maps

$$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \mu \theta_t = (1 - \eta \mu)\,\theta_t .$$

Every update scales the offset by $1 - \eta \mu$, whose sign and size dictate convergence (Figure 2). When $\eta \mu < 1$, $\theta$ steps monotonically toward zero. Between $1 < \eta \mu < 2$, steps hop across zero yet still decay. Past $\eta \mu > 2$, magnitude exceeds one and oscillations grow exponentially. Thus the curvature limit is $2/\mu$: steeper valleys permit smaller steps.

A real network's loss has millions of directions rather than one, and each has its own curvature. These curvatures are the eigenvalues of the loss's Hessian (the matrix of second derivatives), and the largest of them is called the sharpness, $\mu_{\max}$. Near a minimum the loss is close to a quadratic, so gradient descent acts on each eigen-direction separately and the most curved direction is the first to fail. On a quadratic, gradient descent therefore diverges once $\eta > 2/\mu_{\max}$ [2], and I will call $2/\mu_{\max}$ the stability limit.

Why the most curved direction sets the limit

Near a minimum $\theta^\star$, expand $\ell(\theta) \approx \ell(\theta^\star) + \tfrac{1}{2}(\theta - \theta^\star)^\top H (\theta - \theta^\star)$ with Hessian $H$. Writing $\theta - \theta^\star = \sum_i a_i v_i$ in eigenvectors $v_i$ with eigenvalues $\mu_i$, gradient descent updates $a_i \mapsto (1 - \eta\mu_i)\, a_i$ independently. Stability requires $|1 - \eta\mu_i| < 1$ across all $i$, yielding $\eta < 2/\mu_{\max}$.

Momentum shifts the constant 2 by an order-one factor [2]. For adaptive optimizers such as Adam and RMSProp, Cohen et al. (2022) [3] prove an analogous mechanism, Adaptive Edge of Stability: rather than bare Hessian curvature alone (cf. Gilmer et al.'s empirical fit [4]), training equilibrates the maximum eigenvalue of the preconditioned Hessian, letting adaptive methods enter steeper loss valleys by adapting the preconditioner. Treat $2/\mu_{\max}$ as setting the characteristic scale rather than an exact optimizer boundary.

Curvature also evolves over training, connecting two key findings to the Playbook's practice. Under full-batch gradient descent, sharpness rises until it settles near $2/\eta$, hovering at this edge of stability with minor upward blips as loss continues falling [2]. Conversely, models initialized in steep terrain diverge unless optimization nudges weights toward regions with $\mu_{\max} < 2/\eta$ [4]. A gradual learning-rate ramp guides weights into flatter basins safely (explored in the warmup section), though first you must diagnose whether a sweep hits an instability ceiling.

Identifying an unstable workload

To check whether a sweep's winning learning rate reflects a true optimum or a stability cliff, start with existing curves. If top feasible runs press right against infeasible trials (NaNs or "uncharacteristically high values of the loss") whose loss jumps mid-run, the workload is unstable and stabilization should unlock faster convergence. The Playbook names that breakdown boundary the unstable_base_learning_rate. Because the warmup recipe below scales upward from this rate, bracketing it between the highest surviving trial and lowest diverging trial (Figure 4) provides a reliable anchor.

What to try

Faced with divergence, cutting the learning rate is tempting. Yet the Playbook demotes lower rates to a "last resort" because they slow every well-behaved step to survive brief early curvature or rare outlier batches. Figure 5 gathers the full Playbook repair hierarchy.

Because the Playbook's architecture line is brief, literature context helps explain why normalization placement matters. Goyal et al. initialized each residual branch's terminal scale to zero so blocks start as near-identities, smoothing early large-batch steps [6]. Even so, warmup and gradient clipping form the Playbook's primary tunable remedies (Figure 6 and Figure 8).

Learning-rate warmup

When instability hits at step zero because curvature starts high, taking gentle steps first lets the weights settle. That is the role of learning-rate warmup: ramping from zero to the target base rate over $W$ steps (warmup_steps). A linear ramp follows

$$\eta_t = \eta_{\text{base}} \cdot \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{t}{W}\right).$$

Apply warmup whenever a sweep resembles Figure 4, with top feasible trials pressing against rising training losses. Notice how the Playbook sequences tuning in Figure 6: lock in an ambitious target rate first, then search for the shortest ramp that keeps training stable.

Long-horizon Transformer runs qualify that 10% cap: Wortsman et al. (2023) [8] observed warmups up to 25% of training cutting learning-rate sensitivity and lowering final loss.

Figure 7 executes step 4 inside the Figure 4 model. Starting from $u =$ 0.0316 sets a target of $10u \approx 0.316$, while max_train_steps at 100,000 caps the ramp at 10,000 steps. Each candidate runs for one ramp plus twice that duration at target rate, plotting $\eta_t$ against sharpness limit $2/\mu_{\max}$ in panel (a) and stable versus diverging lengths in panel (b).

Why a ramp works and a step does not

At step 0 in this model, curvature sets the ceiling at 0.02, sixteen times below 0.316, so jumping straight to the target rate overshoots immediately. Warmup works because rising steps actively nudge parameters into flatter regions, lifting the stability limit ahead of $\eta_t$. Fast ramps outrun that relaxation and fail, just like the 100- and 300-step schedules in Figure 7.

The "hold, then jump" toggle splits those effects cleanly. Constant low rates push sharpness down only to roughly $2/\eta$. Holding 0.0158 for 1,000 steps leaves curvature stuck near $\mu_0$, so stepping up to 0.316 overshoots by sixteenfold and diverges at step 1,002. On ImageNet ResNet-50, Goyal et al. confirmed this empirically: holding a low constant warmup then jumping raised top-1 error to 25.88% (versus 24.84% without warmup), whereas a linear ramp reached 23.74% [6].

Keep the toy's limits clear: real network curvature follows no closed relaxation rule, and Gilmer et al. report Transformers lacking normalization where warmup alone failed [4].

Gradient clipping

Warmup prevents initial curvature spikes yet cannot rescue runs that blow up deep into training on outlier batches. For mid-run spikes, gradient clipping (global L2 norm rescaling introduced for recurrent networks by Pascanu et al. (2013) [7]) caps each update above threshold $c$:

$$g' = c \, \frac{g}{\lVert g \rVert} \qquad \text{when } \lVert g \rVert > c .$$

Direction stays intact while norm shrinks to $c$. How tight should $c$ be? The Playbook sets it empirically from unclipped norms, placing the cutoff "just above the 'typical' gradient norm" (Figure 8).

Why treat 50% clipping as a hidden learning-rate cut? Because SGD and momentum scale updates linearly with gradients, clipping multiplies any step past $c$ by $c/\lVert g \rVert$. Clip most updates and the mean multiplier shrinks proportionally. Gilmer et al. documented a run clipped at 1.0 against typical norms of 5.0, which in their words was "artificially lowering the learning rate by a factor of 1/5" until they raised $c$ to 6.0 [4].

Figure 9 simulates this across 5,000 unclipped norms combining early elevation with 25 mid-run spikes. At the 90th percentile ($c =$ 1.71), clipping catches 10% of updates and preserves a 0.97 mean scale factor. Cutting $c$ to the median (1.04) clips half of steps and lowers the multiplier to 0.88, while $c =$ 0.40 clips 99% of steps and slices effective step size down to 0.40.

Real runs look messier in detail. In Playbook Figure 9 (an unstable ResNet-200), the plotted curve starts near roughly $10^5$ and falls to about 3 within a few thousand steps, values read by eye from the axis rather than tabulated in the text. Even so, the sizing rule transfers intact: set $c$ just above the bulk distribution to clip outliers alone, and watch the clipped fraction so clipping never quietly cuts your learning rate.

Stabilizing large transformers and low precision

Modern large-scale training pushes beyond warmup and clipping alone, adding structural stabilizers that keep activation and logit magnitudes bounded across depth, width, and reduced precision. Those additions sit alongside the Playbook's core recipes rather than inside the original 2023 document.

  • Pre-norm with RMSNorm. Placing normalization inside residual branches ($x + f(\mathrm{Norm}(x))$) is already in the Playbook (Figure 5), and modern transformers routinely replace standard LayerNorm with RMSNorm (Zhang & Sennrich, 2019) [9], preserving rescaling invariance while dropping mean-centering overhead.
  • QK-LayerNorm and z-loss. To prevent late-stage divergence in wide transformers, Wortsman et al. (2023) [8] apply QK-LayerNorm to queries and keys before attention softmax dots grow unbounded, and pair it with output z-loss ($\alpha\,\log^2 Z$) to keep output logit scales controlled at high learning rates.
  • Use bf16 headroom and fp32 states. Unlike fp16 (5-bit exponent overflowing near 65,504), bf16 retains fp32's 8-bit exponent range (up to ~$3.4 \times 10^{38}$), eliminating dynamic loss-scaling fragile failures while keeping optimizer moments and master weights in fp32 (and tuning AdamW's $\epsilon$ away from tiny defaults like $10^{-8}$ if reduced-precision gradients risk underflow).

In practice

Follow these five steps whenever an edge-crowded sweep flags potential instability, pairing the Playbook's workflow with modern architectural checks.

  1. Verify instability caps your learning rate

    Check whether top feasible sweep trials crowd failing runs whose training curves spike or diverge; otherwise failures mark the top of the useful range (Identifying an unstable workload).

  2. Pinpoint when failures strike

    Run about 500 steps at twice the best rate while logging every step alongside unclipped L2 gradient norms across the run.

    Loss spikes in first steps Start with linear warmup (Warmup recipe).
    Outlier norms mid-run Clip at the 90th norm percentile (Clipping recipe).

  3. Confirm speedup against the baseline

    Prepend warmup or apply clipping at the higher target learning rate, then compare retrospective best checkpoints against the baseline (Part 6).

  4. Widen fixes and harden transformer blocks

    Extend warmup, tighten clipping (staying well under 50% of steps), try Adam, or verify Pre-LN RMSNorm, QK-Norm, z-loss, and bf16 precision with fp32 optimizer state (Modern stabilizers).

  5. Lower the learning rate last

    Only if structural fixes and warmup fail to widen survival should you accept the lower stable step size (What to try).
