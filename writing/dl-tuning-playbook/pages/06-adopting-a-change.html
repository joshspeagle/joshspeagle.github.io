Deciding whether to adopt a change

Josh Speagle Notes on the Playbook's section on adopting a change ↗

Part of a series of notes on Google Research's Deep Learning Tuning Playbook. Read what the Playbook is and how these notes work.

Your study has finished, its plots look healthy, and the best trial beats the baseline by a tenth or two of a percentage point. Should the change go into the baseline? Two training runs of the same configuration rarely give the same validation error, so the answer depends on how the gap compares with that run-to-run noise. The Playbook's advice on the decision is short, and I found it easier to follow once I had pictures of where the noise comes from and of how large a gain has to be before I believe it.

Where the noise comes from

The Playbook opens its section on adopting changes with a situation most people who train networks have met. A candidate change gets a better validation error than the incumbent (the configuration currently serving as the baseline), you repeat the experiment, and the advantage is gone. The authors group the most important causes of such an inconsistent result into broad categories of variation:

  • Training procedure variance. The Playbook also calls it trial variance or retrain variance (Part 4, How many trials?). It is the variation between training runs that use the same hyperparameters with different random seeds, which change the initialization, the order in which training data are shuffled, dropout masks, data augmentation, and the order of parallel arithmetic operations.
  • Hyperparameter search variance. Also called study variance, it is the variation caused by the procedure that selects the hyperparameters. Running the same search space with two seeds for the quasi-random search can end with two different selected configurations.
  • Data collection and sampling variance. This is the variation from a random split into training, validation and test data, or from the process that generates the training data. I shorten it to data variance below.

Figure 1 draws one picture for each source and marks what stays fixed while it varies. The marking matters for the rest of the post, because the usual remedy (rerunning the best trial) varies the seed alone.

The Playbook gives no sizes for these sources in general, but Bouthillier et al. (2021) measured them on five modest benchmarks. Resampling the data was the largest source they measured. Repeating the hyperparameter search added, on average, about as much variance as changing the initialization seed. On CIFAR-10 with a VGG11 network at about 91% accuracy, the initialization seed alone gave a standard deviation of about 0.23 percentage points.

The Playbook adds a warning about statistical tests on the validation set. A test that treats the finite validation set as the only source of randomness can report a significant difference between two trained models that share every hyperparameter and differ only in their seeds. Such a test is a statement about two particular trained networks. The adoption decision concerns configurations, and a configuration's result includes its training randomness.

The best trial looks better than its configuration

Selection adds a bias on top of the noise. A study reports the minimum validation error over many trials. When several trials have hyperparameters that perform about equally well, the minimum tends to pick the one whose seed happened to be lucky. Figure 2 sketches the effect. The winner's first result sits below the error its configuration produces on average, and fresh reruns of the same configuration scatter around that average instead. Rerunning removes the bias because nothing selects the new seeds.

How large the selection bias is

Write the observed error of trial $k$ as $Y_k = L_k + \varepsilon_k$, where $L_k = L(\mathbf{h}_k)$ is the configuration's average over seeds and $\varepsilon_k$ is seed noise with mean zero. The study reports $\min_k Y_k$. The minimum is a concave function, so Jensen's inequality gives

$$\mathbb{E}\Big[\min_k Y_k\Big] \;\le\; \min_k \mathbb{E}[Y_k] \;=\; \min_k L_k .$$

The reported best is therefore optimistic on average. In the worst case all $K$ trials tie, $L_k = L$, and the noise is Gaussian with standard deviation $\sigma$. Then $\mathbb{E}[\min_k Y_k] = L - m_K\,\sigma$, where $m_K$ is the expected maximum of $K$ standard normal draws:

With the Playbook's figure of about 0.1 points of retrain variation for a ResNet-50 on ImageNet, ten tied trials make the winner look about 0.15 points better than its configuration. Real trials do not tie exactly, so only the trials within a few $\sigma$ of the best contribute, and the bias is smaller. A rerun draws a new $\varepsilon$ that no minimum selected, so $\mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{rerun}}] = L_k$ for the chosen trial.

Rerunning the best trial

The Playbook's remedy is direct: "before adopting a candidate change, consider running the best trial N times to characterize the run-to-run trial variance." It adds that you can usually get away with characterizing the trial variance again only after major changes to the pipeline, that some applications need fresher estimates, and that in others a rerun of the best trial costs too much to be worth it. Bouthillier et al. add a practical detail. Reruns that changed only the initialization seed captured less of the variance than reruns that changed every source of randomness, so a rerun should vary the shuffle order, augmentation and dropout seeds as well.

Figure 3 shows what seed noise does to a comparison. Panel (a) draws the runs of a baseline and a candidate. Panel (b) draws the gaps between the means of two identical configurations, simulated 400 times, which is the spread a gap would have if the change did nothing. The shaded band covers two standard errors either side of zero.

At the default setting each configuration has one run, which is the comparison a single tuning study hands you. The standard error of the gap is 0.14 points, so noise produces gaps as large as ±0.28 points, and the observed 0.13 points proves little. With the same true gain stands 2.4 standard errors from zero.

The quantity behind the band is the standard error of the gap. With $N_{\text{b}}$ reruns of the baseline and $N_{\text{c}}$ of the candidate, sample means $\bar{L}_{\text{b}}$ and $\bar{L}_{\text{c}}$, and sample standard deviations $s_{\text{b}}$ and $s_{\text{c}}$ across seeds,

$$\widehat{\Delta} = \bar{L}_{\text{b}} - \bar{L}_{\text{c}}, \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\widehat{\Delta}) = \sqrt{\frac{s_{\text{b}}^2}{N_{\text{b}}} + \frac{s_{\text{c}}^2}{N_{\text{c}}}} .$$

With equal spreads $\sigma$ and equal counts $N$ the standard error is $\sigma\sqrt{2/N}$. Quadrupling the reruns halves it. I treat a gap of about two standard errors as the point where seed noise stops being a comfortable explanation. That two-standard-error threshold is a site convention; the Playbook's qualitative rule is under making the call.

The standard error of a gap, and paired reruns

The mean of $N$ independent runs with standard deviation $\sigma$ has variance $\sigma^2/N$. Runs of the baseline and the candidate are independent of each other, so the variance of the difference of their means is the sum of the two variances:

$$\operatorname{Var}\big(\bar{L}_{\text{b}} - \bar{L}_{\text{c}}\big) = \frac{\sigma_{\text{b}}^2}{N_{\text{b}}} + \frac{\sigma_{\text{c}}^2}{N_{\text{c}}} .$$

Replacing each $\sigma$ with its sample estimate $s$ gives the formula in the text. With few reruns the estimate $s$ is itself noisy (at $N = 2$ it rests on one degree of freedom), so a ratio $\widehat{\Delta}/\mathrm{SE}$ follows a Student $t$ distribution with heavier tails than a normal one, and two standard errors carry less confidence than they would with many runs.

Reruns can also be paired. If rerun $k$ of the baseline and rerun $k$ of the candidate share a seed or a data split, compute the per-pair differences $d_k$ and use $\mathrm{SE} = s_d/\sqrt{N}$, where $s_d$ is the standard deviation of the $d_k$. Because

$$\operatorname{Var}(d_k) = \sigma_{\text{b}}^2 + \sigma_{\text{c}}^2 - 2\rho\,\sigma_{\text{b}}\sigma_{\text{c}},$$

pairing reduces the standard error when the shared randomness makes the two runs of a pair correlated ($\rho > 0$), and changes little when it does not. Bouthillier et al. recommend pairing and base their own decision rule on the probability that the candidate beats the baseline in a paired run. At their chosen error rates that rule needs 29 paired runs per comparison.

What reruns cannot measure

Every rerun of a best trial uses the hyperparameters that one study selected. If the candidate's study happened to sample a point close to its best learning rate and the baseline's study did not, all the reruns inherit that difference. The Playbook says it is "most concerned about study variance when trying to make conclusions that go beyond the level of an individual point in hyperparameters space." It also notes that study variance depends on the number of trials and the search space, and that the authors have seen it both larger and much smaller than trial variance. Their own example is the ResNet-50 on ImageNet in the FAQ on how many trials quasi-random search needs, where even with 20 trials the gap between lucky and unlucky studies is likely larger than the retrain variation of about 0.1 points.

Figure 4 separates the two kinds of luck. Each study's selected configuration carries an offset, drawn once per study, that every rerun of it shares. In the default setting the candidate's study came out 0.02 points above the candidate's average over studies and the baseline's came out 0.17 points above its own. The true gain is 0.2 points. After five reruns of each, the observed gain is 0.30 points with a standard error of 0.07, or 4.1 standard errors. The reruns are precise about the two selected configurations and say nothing about the extra 0.15 points of search luck. Pressing "Repeat the study" runs the candidate's whole search again and plots its new best trial, and the spread of those points shows the luck that reruns cannot.

Whether search luck matters depends on the claim. If the claim is "this configuration beats that one", reruns measure the right thing, and the selected points are what you would ship. If the claim is about a change in general (a new schedule beats the old one when both are well tuned), study variance enters, and the protections are a search dense enough to pass the checks in Part 5, Have you sampled enough points?, a fair amount of tuning for the baseline, and, when the budget allows, repeating the study.

Making the call

With the noise measured as well as you can afford, the Playbook's rule for adopting a change is lenient. The authors write that "demanding complete certainty that something helps isn't the right answer either," and continue:

if a new hyperparameter point (or other change) gets a better result than the baseline (taking into account the retrain variance of both the new point and the baseline as best we can), then we probably should adopt it as the new baseline for future comparisons.

The leniency makes sense once you remember what adoption means here. The new baseline is the reference point for the next round of the incremental strategy, and the next round will test it again. A wrong adoption of a small, simple change costs one round's comparisons. Waiting for certainty on every change would slow every round that follows.

The Playbook attaches one condition: "we should only adopt changes that produce improvements that outweigh any complexity they add." Complexity is whatever the change asks of every later experiment. Examples I would count include extra code paths, new hyperparameters that later studies must tune, slower steps and more memory. In my view, a real gain of a few hundredths of a point rarely justifies a new tuned hyperparameter, while a change that simplifies the pipeline at equal error is worth adopting with no gain.

After exploration concludes

Everything so far belongs to the exploration phase, where the goal of each round is insight into the tuning problem. The Playbook's section on what happens after exploration covers the switch to producing a single best configuration to launch or otherwise use. By then you should have a refined search space that comfortably contains the region around the best observed trial and has been sampled adequately, and exploration should have revealed which hyperparameters matter and sensible ranges for them. Figure 5 sets the two phases side by side.

The switch of search algorithm follows from the change of goal. Quasi-random search suits exploration because its properties serve insight, such as uniform coverage that keeps axis plots readable and freedom to change the objective after the fact (the list is in Part 4, Why not Bayesian optimization?). Once insight no longer matters, the Playbook says many of those advantages no longer apply, and Bayesian optimization tools should find the best configuration automatically.

The final study needs care with divergent trials and with the test set. If the search space still contains a non-trivial volume of divergent points (NaN training loss, or loss many standard deviations worse than the mean), the optimizer must handle such trials properly. The Playbook points to Gelbart et al. (2014) for a good method and notes that Vizier supports marking trials infeasible, although its approach depends on how it is configured. The end of exploration is also the point to consider checking performance on the test set. The authors mention that one could even fold the validation set into the training set and retrain the best configuration, but only when there will be no future launches with this workload, such as a one-time Kaggle competition.

In practice

I follow this sequence when a study's best trial beats the baseline. Steps 1, 4, 5 and 6 restate the Playbook. Steps 2 and 3 are my reading of it, and the threshold in step 2 is my own convention.

  1. Rerun the best trial and the baseline

    Run each N times with fresh seeds, varying every source of randomness you control. Reuse a recent estimate of the trial variance unless the pipeline changed substantially. Playbook; Bouthillier et al.

  2. Compare the gap with its standard error

    Compute $\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{s_{\text{b}}^2/N_{\text{b}} + s_{\text{c}}^2/N_{\text{c}}}$. A gap near two standard errors or more is hard to explain by seed noise. Remember that the first result of the best trial is optimistic. My reading

  3. Check the search behind each configuration

    For a claim about a change in general, confirm both studies were dense and fair, since reruns cannot see search luck. Repeat the study when the budget allows. My reading

  4. Weigh the complexity

    Adopt only if the improvement outweighs the code, hyperparameters, time and memory the change adds. Playbook

  5. Adopt without demanding certainty

    A change that beats the baseline after allowing for both configurations' retrain variance probably becomes the new baseline. The next round will test it again. Playbook

  6. When exploration ends, run one final study

    Use Bayesian optimization over the refined search space with the largest budget you can, handle divergent trials, then consider the test set. Playbook
