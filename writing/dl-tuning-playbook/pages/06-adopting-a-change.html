Deciding whether to adopt a change

Josh Speagle Notes on the Playbook's section on adopting a change ↗

Part of a series of notes on Google Research's Deep Learning Tuning Playbook. Read what the Playbook is and how these notes work.

Suppose a study has finished, its plots look healthy, and the best trial beats the baseline by a tenth or two of a percentage point. Should we fold the change into the baseline? Two training runs of the same configuration rarely give the same validation error, so the answer depends on how that gap compares with the run-to-run noise. The Playbook's advice on this decision is brief, and I found it much easier to follow once I had a picture of where the noise comes from and of how large a gain has to be before I believe it. So we'll start by separating the sources of variation and the optimism that comes from picking a winner, then see how rerunning the best trial shrinks the noise and what it leaves unmeasured, before turning to the adoption rule itself and to the final study that ends the exploration phase.

Where the noise comes from

Most people who train networks have met the situation that opens the Playbook's section on adopting changes. A candidate change beats the incumbent (the configuration currently serving as the baseline) on validation error, we repeat the experiment, and the advantage is gone. To make sense of why, Godbole et al. sort the most important causes of such inconsistent results into broad categories of variation:

  • Training procedure variance, which the Playbook also calls trial variance or retrain variance (Part 4, How many trials?). This is the spread between runs that share their hyperparameters but use different random seeds, and so differ in initialization, the order in which training data are shuffled, dropout masks, data augmentation, and the order of parallel arithmetic operations.
  • Hyperparameter search variance, or study variance, which comes from the procedure that selects the hyperparameters. Running a quasi-random search over the same space with two different seeds can end with two different selected configurations.
  • Data collection and sampling variance, which comes from a random split into training, validation and test data, or from the process that generates the training data. I'll shorten this to data variance below.

Figure 1 draws one picture for each source and marks what stays fixed while it varies. That bookkeeping will matter for the rest of the post, because the usual remedy, rerunning the best trial, varies only the seed.

How large are these sources in practice? The Playbook doesn't give general sizes, but Bouthillier et al. (2021) measured them on five modest benchmarks. Resampling the data was the largest source they found, while repeating the hyperparameter search added, on average, about as much variance as changing the initialization seed. On CIFAR-10 with a VGG11 network at about 91% accuracy, the initialization seed alone gave a standard deviation of about 0.23 percentage points.

This framing also explains a warning in the Playbook about statistical tests on the validation set. A test that treats the finite validation set as the only source of randomness can declare a significant difference between two trained models that share every hyperparameter and differ only in their seeds. That test is answering a question about two particular trained networks, whereas the adoption decision is about configurations, and a configuration's result includes its training randomness.

Why the best trial looks better than its configuration

Noise is only half of the problem, because the act of selection adds a bias on top of it. A study reports the minimum validation error over many trials, and when several trials have hyperparameters that perform about equally well, the minimum tends to land on whichever one drew a lucky seed. This is a form of the winner's curse, a property of the order statistics of noisy draws, and Figure 2 sketches it. The winner's first result sits below the error its configuration produces on average, while fresh reruns of the same configuration scatter around that average instead. Rerunning removes the bias precisely because nothing selects the new seeds.

How large the selection bias is

Let's write the observed error of trial $k$ as $Y_k = L_k + \varepsilon_k$, where $L_k = L(\mathbf{h}_k)$ is the configuration's average over seeds and $\varepsilon_k$ is seed noise with mean zero. The study reports $\min_k Y_k$, and because the minimum is a concave function, Jensen's inequality gives

$$\mathbb{E}\Big[\min_k Y_k\Big] \;\le\; \min_k \mathbb{E}[Y_k] \;=\; \min_k L_k .$$

So the reported best is optimistic on average. To see how optimistic, take the worst case, in which all $K$ trials tie at $L_k = L$ and the noise is Gaussian with standard deviation $\sigma$. Then $\mathbb{E}[\min_k Y_k] = L - m_K\,\sigma$, where $m_K$ is the expected maximum of $K$ standard normal draws:

Taking the Playbook's figure of about 0.1 points of retrain variation for a ResNet-50 on ImageNet, ten tied trials make the winner look about 0.15 points better than its configuration. Real trials do not tie exactly, so only those within a few $\sigma$ of the best contribute and the bias is smaller. A rerun, by contrast, draws a new $\varepsilon$ that no minimum selected, so $\mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{rerun}}] = L_k$ for the chosen trial.

Rerunning the best trial

If both the noise and the selection bias live in the seed, the natural remedy is to draw more seeds, and the Playbook recommends that "before adopting a candidate change, consider running the best trial N times to characterize the run-to-run trial variance." The authors qualify this in three ways. We can usually get away with re-estimating the trial variance only after major changes to the pipeline, some applications need fresher estimates, and in others a rerun of the best trial costs too much to be worth it. Bouthillier et al. add a practical detail, finding that reruns that changed only the initialization seed captured less of the variance than reruns that changed every source of randomness, so a rerun should also vary the shuffle order, augmentation and dropout seeds.

To see what seed noise does to a comparison, consider Figure 3. Panel (a) draws the runs of a baseline and a candidate, while panel (b) simulates the gap between the means of two identical configurations 400 times, which is the spread we would see if the change did nothing at all. The shaded band covers two standard errors on either side of zero.

The default setting gives each configuration a single run, which is exactly the comparison a single tuning study hands us. The standard error of the gap is then 0.14 points, so noise alone produces gaps as large as ±0.28 points and the observed 0.13 points tells us little. With , the same true gain stands 2.4 standard errors from zero.

The quantity behind the band is the standard error of the gap, which is worth writing down because it is what we actually compute from reruns. With $N_{\text{b}}$ reruns of the baseline and $N_{\text{c}}$ of the candidate, sample means $\bar{L}_{\text{b}}$ and $\bar{L}_{\text{c}}$, and sample standard deviations $s_{\text{b}}$ and $s_{\text{c}}$ across seeds,

$$\widehat{\Delta} = \bar{L}_{\text{b}} - \bar{L}_{\text{c}}, \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\widehat{\Delta}) = \sqrt{\frac{s_{\text{b}}^2}{N_{\text{b}}} + \frac{s_{\text{c}}^2}{N_{\text{c}}}} .$$

With equal spreads $\sigma$ and equal counts $N$, the standard error reduces to $\sigma\sqrt{2/N}$, which means that quadrupling the reruns only halves it. I treat a gap of about two standard errors as the point where seed noise stops being a comfortable explanation. That threshold is my own convention for these notes rather than the Playbook's, whose qualitative rule we'll come to in making the call.

The standard error of a gap, and paired reruns

The mean of $N$ independent runs with standard deviation $\sigma$ has variance $\sigma^2/N$. Runs of the baseline and the candidate are independent of each other, so the variance of the difference of their means is the sum of the two variances:

$$\operatorname{Var}\big(\bar{L}_{\text{b}} - \bar{L}_{\text{c}}\big) = \frac{\sigma_{\text{b}}^2}{N_{\text{b}}} + \frac{\sigma_{\text{c}}^2}{N_{\text{c}}} .$$

Replacing each $\sigma$ with its sample estimate $s$ gives the formula in the text. With few reruns, though, the estimate $s$ is itself noisy (at $N = 2$ it rests on one degree of freedom), so the ratio $\widehat{\Delta}/\mathrm{SE}$ follows a Student $t$ distribution with heavier tails than a normal one, and two standard errors carry less confidence than they would with many runs.

We can also pair the reruns. If rerun $k$ of the baseline and rerun $k$ of the candidate share a seed or a data split, we compute the per-pair differences $d_k$ and use $\mathrm{SE} = s_d/\sqrt{N}$, where $s_d$ is the standard deviation of the $d_k$. Because

$$\operatorname{Var}(d_k) = \sigma_{\text{b}}^2 + \sigma_{\text{c}}^2 - 2\rho\,\sigma_{\text{b}}\sigma_{\text{c}},$$

pairing reduces the standard error when the shared randomness makes the two runs of a pair correlated ($\rho > 0$), and changes little when it does not. Bouthillier et al. recommend pairing, and they base their own decision rule on the probability that the candidate beats the baseline in a paired run; at their chosen error rates, that rule needs 29 paired runs per comparison.

What reruns cannot measure

Reruns fix the seed problem, but they leave something untouched. Every rerun of a best trial uses the hyperparameters that one particular study selected, so if the candidate's study happened to sample a point close to its best learning rate while the baseline's study did not, all the reruns inherit that difference. This is why the Playbook is "most concerned about study variance when trying to make conclusions that go beyond the level of an individual point in hyperparameters space." How much it matters depends on the number of trials and the search space, and the authors report having seen study variance both larger and much smaller than trial variance. Their own example is the ResNet-50 on ImageNet in the FAQ on how many trials quasi-random search needs, where even with 20 trials the gap between lucky and unlucky studies is likely larger than the retrain variation of about 0.1 points.

To separate the two kinds of luck, I built Figure 4 so that each study's selected configuration carries an offset, drawn once per study and shared by every rerun of it. In the default setting the candidate's study came out 0.02 points above the candidate's average over studies, while the baseline's came out 0.17 points above its own. The true gain is 0.2 points, yet after five reruns of each the observed gain is 0.30 points with a standard error of 0.07, or 4.1 standard errors. The reruns are precise about the two selected configurations, but they say nothing about the extra 0.15 points of search luck. Pressing "Repeat the study" runs the candidate's whole search again and plots its new best trial, and the spread of those points shows us the luck that reruns cannot see.

Whether search luck matters, then, depends on what we are claiming. If the claim is "this configuration beats that one", reruns measure the right thing, because the selected points are what we would actually ship. If instead the claim is about a change in general (a new schedule beats the old one when both are well tuned), study variance enters the comparison. In that case I think the protections are a search dense enough to pass the checks in Part 5, Have we sampled enough points?, a fair amount of tuning for the baseline, and, when the budget allows, repeating the study.

Making the call

Once we have measured the noise as well as we can afford, how sure do we need to be? Perhaps surprisingly, the Playbook's rule for adopting a change is lenient. The authors write that "demanding complete certainty that something helps isn't the right answer either," and continue:

if a new hyperparameter point (or other change) gets a better result than the baseline (taking into account the retrain variance of both the new point and the baseline as best we can), then we probably should adopt it as the new baseline for future comparisons.

The leniency makes sense once we remember what adoption means here. The new baseline is only the reference point for the next round of the incremental strategy, and that round will test it again. Wrongly adopting a small, simple change costs us one round's comparisons, whereas waiting for certainty on every change would slow down every round that follows. This is an asymmetric-loss argument, and in a sequential loop it favours acting on the evidence we have.

The rule does come with one condition: "we should only adopt changes that produce improvements that outweigh any complexity they add." I find it useful to think of complexity as whatever the change asks of every later experiment, which would include extra code paths, new hyperparameters that later studies must tune, slower steps and more memory. In my view, a real gain of a few hundredths of a point rarely justifies a new tuned hyperparameter, while a change that simplifies the pipeline at equal error is worth adopting even with no gain.

After exploration concludes

Everything so far belongs to the exploration phase, where the goal of each round is insight into the tuning problem. Eventually, though, the rounds have taught us what they can, and the goal changes to producing a single best configuration to launch or otherwise use. The Playbook's section on what happens after exploration describes this switch. By that point we should have a refined search space that comfortably contains the region around the best observed trial and has been sampled adequately, and exploration should have revealed which hyperparameters matter and sensible ranges for them. Figure 5 sets the two phases side by side.

The change of search algorithm follows directly from the change of goal. Quasi-random search suits exploration because its properties serve insight, such as uniform coverage that keeps axis plots readable and the freedom to change the objective after the fact (the full list is in Part 4, Why not Bayesian optimization?). Once insight no longer matters, many of those advantages stop applying, and the Playbook's view is that Bayesian optimization tools should then find the best configuration automatically.

Two details of the final study need care. The first is divergent trials. If the search space still contains a non-trivial volume of divergent points (NaN training loss, or loss many standard deviations worse than the mean), the optimizer has to handle such trials properly, and the Playbook points to Gelbart et al. (2014) for a good method while noting that Vizier supports marking trials infeasible, although its approach depends on how it is configured. The second is the test set, since the end of exploration is the natural point to consider checking performance on it. The authors even mention folding the validation set into the training set and retraining the best configuration, but only when there will be no future launches with this workload, such as a one-time Kaggle competition.

In practice

Putting the pieces together, this is the sequence I follow when a study's best trial beats the baseline. Each step is tagged below to distinguish the Playbook's rules from my own conventions.

  1. Rerun the best trial and the baseline

    Run each N times with fresh seeds, varying every source of randomness we control. Reuse a recent estimate of the trial variance unless the pipeline changed substantially. Playbook; Bouthillier et al.

  2. Compare the gap with its standard error

    Compute $\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{s_{\text{b}}^2/N_{\text{b}} + s_{\text{c}}^2/N_{\text{c}}}$. A gap of about two standard errors or more is hard to explain by seed noise. Keep in mind that the best trial's first result is optimistic. My reading

  3. Check the search behind each configuration

    For a claim about a change in general, confirm both studies were dense and fair, since reruns cannot see search luck. Repeat the study when the budget allows. My reading

  4. Weigh the complexity

    Adopt only if the improvement outweighs the code, hyperparameters, time and memory the change adds. Playbook

  5. Adopt without demanding certainty

    A change that beats the baseline after allowing for both configurations' retrain variance probably becomes the new baseline. The next round will test it again. Playbook

  6. When exploration ends, run one final study

    Use Bayesian optimization over the refined search space with the largest budget we can, handle divergent trials, then consider the test set. Playbook
