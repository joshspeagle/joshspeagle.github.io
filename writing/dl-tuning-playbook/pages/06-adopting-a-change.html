Deciding whether to adopt a change

Josh Speagle Notes on the Playbook's section on adopting a change ↗

Part of a series of notes on Google Research's Deep Learning Tuning Playbook. Read what the Playbook is and how these notes work.

Suppose a tuning study finishes, its diagnostics pass, and the winner trims validation error by a tenth or two of a point. Should you adopt that change into the baseline? Training the same config twice rarely returns identical validation scores, so the answer rests on signal versus noise. Below I separate training, search, and data variance, quantify selection optimism, measure how reruns narrow error bars, and trace the path from lenient exploration adoption to the final Bayesian optimization run.

In brief
  • Expect an optimistic study winner: Minimum selection picks lucky seeds; fresh reruns remove selection bias and recover true mean error (Where the noise comes from).
  • Vary every seed during reruns: Sweep fresh initialization, batch shuffle, dropout, and augmentation seeds across $N$ runs, or pair seeds to cut standard error (Rerunning the best trial).
  • Remember search luck stays hidden from reruns: Repeating a point measures trial variance alone; checking study variance takes dense search or repeating the sweep (What reruns cannot measure).
  • Adopt leniently inside exploration: Accept clear improvements after accounting for seed noise unless complexity outweighs the gain, then finish with Bayesian optimization (Making the call).

Where the noise comes from

Everyone who trains networks eventually runs into the puzzle opening the Playbook's [3] section on adopting changes: a candidate beats the current baseline, you repeat the experiment, and the margin vanishes. Godbole et al. organize those random fluctuations into three broad categories of variation:

  • Training procedure variance (also called trial variance or retrain variance, Part 4): spread across runs sharing identical hyperparameters under fresh random seeds (weight initialization, batch order, dropout masks, augmentation draws, and parallel reduction order).
  • Hyperparameter search variance (study variance): variation induced by the search sequence itself, where rerunning quasi-random exploration on two search seeds chooses two slightly different optima.
  • Data collection and sampling variance (shortened here to data variance): variability from finite training/validation/test splits and data sampling.

Figure 1 diagrams what each noise source holds fixed versus varies. That accounting matters later because rerunning a single winner probes only training variance.

How large are those sources empirically? While the Playbook gives no general bounds, Bouthillier et al. (2021) [1] measured them across five vision and language benchmarks. Resampling data splits was the largest contributor overall, while search variation matched initialization noise on average; on CIFAR-10 VGG11 (~91% accuracy), initialization seed alone showed a standard deviation of 0.23 percentage points.

This explains the Playbook's warning against paired McNemar-style significance tests conditioned on one fixed validation split. Treating test-set sampling as the only randomness can flag two identical hyperparameter runs trained from different seeds as "significantly different," answering a question about two frozen checkpoints rather than the repeatability of a training pipeline.

Why the best trial looks better than its configuration

Variance alone is only half the trap because minimum selection adds downward bias. When several configurations perform similarly near the valley floor, picking the lowest observed validation error favors whichever configuration drew an unusually lucky seed (Figure 2). Fresh reruns sample unconditioned seeds centered back around that configuration's true average error.

How large the selection bias is

Write trial $k$'s observed error as $Y_k = L_k + \varepsilon_k$, where $L_k = L(\mathbf{h}_k)$ is its true mean across seeds and $\varepsilon_k$ has mean zero. Reporting $\min_k Y_k$ puts concave minimization outside expectation, so Jensen's inequality gives

$$\mathbb{E}\Big[\min_k Y_k\Big] \;\le\; \min_k \mathbb{E}[Y_k] \;=\; \min_k L_k .$$

Thus the best observed run is optimistic on average. In the boundary case where $K$ configurations tie at $L$ with Gaussian noise of standard deviation $\sigma$, $\mathbb{E}[\min_k Y_k] = L - m_K\,\sigma$, where $m_K$ is the expected maximum of $K$ standard normals:

Taking the Playbook's ImageNet ResNet-50 figure of $\sigma_{\text{trial}} \approx 0.1$ points, ten tied trials inflate the winner by roughly 0.15 points (error reads $-0.15$ low relative to true expectation). Real sweeps spread trials away from ties so bias is smaller; independent reruns draw fresh unconditioned noise with $\mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{rerun}}] = L_k$.

Rerunning the best trial

Since both trial variance and selection bias live in the random seed, rerunning cures both. The Playbook advises: "before adopting a candidate change, consider running the best trial N times to characterize the run-to-run trial variance." You do not need full $N$-seed sweeps on every minor tweak; re-estimate trial variance after major pipeline edits or on high-stakes launches when budgets permit. Bouthillier et al. [1] emphasize that varying initialization alone understates variance, so a proper rerun of the best trial must resample shuffle order, data augmentation, and dropout seeds together.

Figure 3 illustrates how seed spread controls detectability. Panel (a) draws baseline and candidate reruns, while panel (b) simulates 400 null differences at $\sigma_{\text{trial}} = 0.1$ points with a $\pm 2\,\mathrm{SE}$ noise band centered at zero.

With one run each ($N=1$), standard error is 0.14 points and zero-difference noise routinely spans $\pm$0.28 points, swamping the observed 0.13-point gain. Moving to pushes the same signal 2.4 standard errors past zero.

To quantify that band from real reruns of baseline ($N_{\text{b}}$ runs, mean $\bar{L}_{\text{b}}$, SD $s_{\text{b}}$) and candidate ($N_{\text{c}}$, $\bar{L}_{\text{c}}$, $s_{\text{c}}$), compute:

$$\widehat{\Delta} = \bar{L}_{\text{b}} - \bar{L}_{\text{c}}, \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\widehat{\Delta}) = \sqrt{\frac{s_{\text{b}}^2}{N_{\text{b}}} + \frac{s_{\text{c}}^2}{N_{\text{c}}}} .$$

With equal counts $N$ and noise $\sigma$, $\mathrm{SE} = \sigma\sqrt{2/N}$, meaning quadrupling compute only halves error bars. As a personal convention in these notes, a gap around two standard errors signals noise alone is an unlikely explanation; the Playbook states its own qualitative adoption rule in Making the call.

The standard error of a gap, and paired reruns

An average over $N$ independent seeds with standard deviation $\sigma$ has variance $\sigma^2/N$. Independent baseline and candidate batches add variances directly:

$$\operatorname{Var}\big(\bar{L}_{\text{b}} - \bar{L}_{\text{c}}\big) = \frac{\sigma_{\text{b}}^2}{N_{\text{b}}} + \frac{\sigma_{\text{c}}^2}{N_{\text{c}}} .$$

Substituting sample standard deviations $s_{\text{b}}, s_{\text{c}}$ yields the text formula. When $N$ is tiny (say $N=2$), noisy $s$ gives $\widehat{\Delta}/\mathrm{SE}$ heavier Student-$t$ tails, weakening two-SE bounds.

Pairing reruns helps when pipeline seeds share variance: if pair $k$ shares initialization/split seeds with difference $d_k$, compute $\mathrm{SE} = s_d/\sqrt{N}$, where

$$\operatorname{Var}(d_k) = \sigma_{\text{b}}^2 + \sigma_{\text{c}}^2 - 2\rho\,\sigma_{\text{b}}\sigma_{\text{c}}.$$

Positive pair correlation ($\rho > 0$) shrinks variance cleanly. Bouthillier et al. advise pairing and estimate 29 paired runs per comparison under their error-rate threshold rule (far more than everyday exploration rounds afford).

What reruns cannot measure

Reruns measure seed variance cleanly, yet freeze the hyperparameters returned by one search. If the candidate's sweep stumbled close to its true optimum while the baseline's sweep missed slightly, every seed rerun inherits that offset. That is why the Playbook stays "most concerned about study variance when trying to make conclusions that go beyond the level of an individual point in hyperparameters space." On their ImageNet ResNet-50 benchmark (FAQ on trial counts ↗), even 20-trial study variation (about 0.3 points SD, inferred from a 0.4-point IQR) exceeds the $\sigma_{\text{trial}} = 0.1$ retrain spread.

Figure 4 separates those two variances by drawing one search offset per study shared across its reruns. At defaults, the candidate's study scored 0.02 above mean versus 0.17 for the baseline, inflating a true 0.20-point gain into an observed 0.30 $\pm$ 0.07 (4.1 SE). Press "Repeat the study" to expose the search luck five reruns could not see.

Whether search luck enters your conclusion depends on what you claim. To ship one concrete config, seed reruns measure the right uncertainty. To claim a general method beats another when tuned cleanly, confirm dense symmetric searches (Part 5) or repeat studies if compute allows. For expensive model or optimizer edits, modern scaling workflows often test candidates on narrow proxy widths parameterized with $\mu$P [4] so multi-seed reruns finish cheaply before zero-shot transfer to full width.

Making the call

Once seed noise is bounded, how confident must you be? Surprisingly, the Playbook's criterion for adopting a change leans permissive: "demanding complete certainty that something helps isn't the right answer either":

The Playbook on adopting changes ↗

if a new hyperparameter point (or other change) gets a better result than the baseline (taking into account the retrain variance of both the new point and the baseline as best we can), then we probably should adopt it as the new baseline for future comparisons.

That leniency reflects what adoption means inside an iterative loop: the updated config merely seeds the next round rather than shipping permanently. Falsely adopting a zero-gain tweak wastes one exploratory comparison, whereas demanding strict proof on every iteration stalls progress. In a sequential loop, asymmetric costs favor acting on solid provisional evidence.

One guardrail offsets that leniency: "we should only adopt changes that produce improvements that outweigh any complexity they add." Complexity means persistent ongoing drag: extra branching code, new nuisance parameters every later sweep must tune, slower throughput, or higher activation RAM. Gains under a few hundredths of a point rarely pay back another tuned hyperparameter, whereas simplifications that tie on error belong in right away.

After exploration concludes

Everything above serves exploration, where rounds build insight. Once rounds reveal which knobs move validation loss and bracket their best regions (Figure 5), the Playbook switches to producing a single best configuration (after exploration ↗).

Switching search tools matches the goal shift. Quasi-random search excels while building axis plots and re-scoring objectives (Part 4); once exploration ends, turn optimization over to Bayesian search.

Mind two final details before launching the grand sweep. First, handle infeasible runs properly: if divergences still occur inside the refined box, configure your Bayesian tool to mark NaN or extreme-loss trials infeasible using constraint handling such as Gelbart et al. (2014) [2] (supported in Vizier depending on configuration). Second, evaluate your held-out test set at the end, folding the validation set into final training only for one-off workloads without future tuning rounds (such as fixed competitions).

In practice

Follow these six steps whenever a candidate beats the baseline; source tags distinguish Playbook rules from conventions added in these notes.

  1. Rerun the best trial and the baseline Playbook; Bouthillier et al.

    Train each $N$ times across fresh seeds varying initialization, batch order, augmentation, and dropout. Reuse recent trial-variance estimates if pipeline noise stayed unchanged.

  2. Compare the gap with its standard error This post

    Compute $\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{s_{\text{b}}^2/N_{\text{b}} + s_{\text{c}}^2/N_{\text{c}}}$. A margin past two standard errors resists seed noise; discount un-rerun study winners for minimum-selection optimism.

  3. Check the search behind each configuration This post

    To support general claims beyond one config pair, confirm dense symmetric studies in Part 5 or repeat the search when budget allows.

  4. Weigh the complexity Playbook

    Adopt only when validated gains clearly outweigh added code paths, extra nuisance hyperparameters, step latency, or memory pressure.

  5. Adopt without demanding certainty Playbook

    If the candidate beats the baseline after weighing both retrain variances, make it the new baseline and let later rounds retest it.

  6. When exploration ends, run one final study Playbook

    Deploy Bayesian optimization across your narrowed space with full trial budget, mark infeasible points cleanly, and evaluate on the held-out test set afterward.
