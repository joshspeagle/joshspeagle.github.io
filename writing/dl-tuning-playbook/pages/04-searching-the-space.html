Where to put your trials, and how many to run

Josh Speagle Notes on the Playbook's section on quasi-random search ↗

Part of a series of notes on Google Research's Deep Learning Tuning Playbook. Read what the Playbook is and how these notes work.

Once you know which hyperparameters a study will tune and over what ranges, you still have to decide where in those ranges each trial goes, and how many trials to run. The familiar answer, a regular grid, spends a small budget badly. The Playbook recommends quasi-random search while you are still learning about a problem, and it says plainly that nobody can tell you in general how many trials are enough. I wanted to see for myself why both statements hold.

Why a grid wastes trials

A study runs a set of trials over a search space, so it needs a rule for placing its $K$ trials. If you are tuning the learning rate and the weight decay, the most familiar rule is a grid. You pick a few values of each and try every combination. A grid feels thorough because it covers every combination evenly.

In practice, hyperparameters rarely matter equally. Bergstra and Bengio found that on most of the datasets they studied, only a few hyperparameters had much effect on performance, and different ones mattered on different datasets. Suppose validation error depends mostly on the learning rate. places nine trials both ways and projects them onto the learning rate.

The three trials in each grid column differ only in weight decay, which barely changes the result. The grid has paid for nine trials and learned about three learning rates. Random search, which draws each coordinate of each trial independently and uniformly, tests nine different learning rates for the same cost.

The waste grows with the number of hyperparameters. A grid with $k$ values per hyperparameter in $d$ dimensions has $k^d$ points, so a budget of $K$ trials allows only $k = \lfloor K^{1/d} \rfloor$ values of each. Sixteen trials over four hyperparameters test two learning rates. The Playbook accepts grid search in one or two dimensions and advises against it in higher dimensions, citing the same paper (the Playbook's section on implementations ↗).

Even coverage and quasi-random search

Random search fixes the repeated values, but it has a weakness of its own. Independent random draws clump. By chance some trials fall close together, and a wide stretch of the axis goes untested. A simple measure of this is the largest gap, the widest stretch of an axis that contains no trial. I find it the most useful single number for coverage, because the best setting could sit anywhere inside that stretch.

The random draw in leaves a gap about three times the even spacing, and that draw is typical. Its gap of 0.19 sits at the median of 1,000 random draws of 16 trials (0.19).

Quasi-random search places trials along a low-discrepancy sequence, a deterministic sequence built so that every prefix of it is spread out evenly. The Playbook describes it as "jittered, shuffled grid search", since it "uniformly, but randomly, explores a given search space and spreads out the search points more than random search" (the Playbook's section on quasi-random search ↗).

The implementations the Playbook links generate a Halton sequence. For one hyperparameter, the Halton sequence counts in base 2 and reads the digits of the count backwards after the binary point. The first trials therefore fall at $1/2$, then $1/4$ and $3/4$, then the eighths, each one splitting the largest remaining gap. Each further hyperparameter uses a different prime base (3, 5, 7 and so on). The Playbook says the implementations are intended to scramble the digits and shift the whole set at random, as recommended by Bousquet et al. , so that two studies with different seeds get different points.

In four dimensions the grid's largest gap in surprised me. It stays at 0.5 from 16 trials to 80, because the next full grid needs 81 trials. The quasi-random gap also surprised me. It falls in steps, roughly halving each time the number of trials passes a power of two, while the random gap shrinks slowly and unevenly.

The advantage of quasi-random over random search is real but modest, and it narrows as the number of hyperparameters grows. In Bergstra and Bengio's simulations, low-discrepancy sequences held some advantage over random points in low dimensions (about one to five), and with many hyperparameters relative to the budget they did not expect a low-discrepancy set to behave very differently from a random one . The Playbook's advice matches this. If no quasi-random implementation is at hand, uniform random search is an acceptable substitute, though it is "likely to be slightly less efficient".

The expected largest gap of random search

Drop $K$ independent uniform points on $[0, 1]$. They cut the interval into $K + 1$ gaps (the spacings), counting the two end pieces. A classical result for the largest spacing $\Delta_{\max}$ is

$$\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{\max}] = \frac{H_{K+1}}{K+1},$$

where $H_n = 1 + \tfrac12 + \dots + \tfrac1n$ is the $n$-th harmonic number. One way to see it is that the spacings have the same joint distribution as $n = K + 1$ independent exponential variables divided by their sum, the largest of $n$ unit exponentials has mean $H_n$, and the normalized vector is independent of the sum, whose mean is $n$.

Evenly spaced points give $1/(K+1)$, so random search's largest gap is on average $H_{K+1}$ times the even spacing. Since $H_n \approx \ln n + \gamma_{\text{EM}}$, with $\gamma_{\text{EM}} \approx 0.577$ the Euler–Mascheroni constant,

$$\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{\max}] \approx \frac{\ln(K+1) + 0.577}{K+1}.$$

At $K = 16$ the ratio is $H_{17} \approx 3.4$ and the expected gap is about 0.20, close to the median of 0.19 over the simulated draws (the distribution has a long upper tail, so its mean sits above its median). The ratio grows like $\ln K$, so random search falls further behind even spacing as the budget grows, but slowly.

How the Halton sequence spreads its points

Write the trial index $i$ in base $b$ as $i = \sum_j a_j b^j$. The radical inverse reflects the digits about the point,

$$\phi_b(i) = \sum_j a_j\, b^{-j-1},$$

and the $i$-th Halton point is $(\phi_2(i), \phi_3(i), \phi_5(i), \dots)$, one prime base per hyperparameter. In base 2, the indices $0, \dots, 2^m - 1$ map onto every multiple of $2^{-m}$, so after each power of two the learning rates are evenly spaced. Between powers of two the new points fill the old gaps in turn, which gives the steps in panel (d) of .

Bousquet et al. scramble the digits with a fixed permutation and add a random shift, which makes the points random while keeping them spread out. Their first choice is a scrambled, shifted Hammersley set, and in their analysis the scrambled, shifted Halton sequence has the same desirable properties. Hammersley needs the number of trials fixed in advance, while Halton can be extended one trial at a time, which suits the implementations the Playbook links. Bousquet et al. also show that the projected spread of a grid onto the important hyperparameters shrinks like $K^{-1/d}$, while for random and Halton points it depends only on the number of important hyperparameters, a formal version of . They recommend giving the smallest bases to the most important hyperparameters, because a coordinate with a large base $b$ fills its axis evenly only once the number of trials exceeds $b$.

Why not Bayesian optimization?

Quasi-random search decides every trial's hyperparameters before any trial has finished. An adaptive search method uses finished trials to steer later ones toward promising regions. Bayesian optimization, for example, fits a statistical model to the results so far and uses it to choose the next trials , and evolutionary algorithms breed new trials from good old ones. Adaptive search sounds strictly better, so I was surprised that the Playbook prefers quasi-random search for most of the tuning process.

The Playbook's answer depends on what a study is for. It separates exploration, where the goal is insight into the tuning problem, from exploitation, where the goal is one best configuration. For exploration it lists these advantages of quasi-random search over adaptive tools (the Playbook's section on quasi-random search ↗).

  1. Change the objective afterward

    No trial's placement depended on earlier results, so you can rank the same trials by another criterion without rerunning anything. The Playbook's examples are final validation error, training error or another evaluation metric, in place of the usual best validation error at any point in training.

  2. Reproducible studies

    A study from six months ago can be reproduced even if the search code changes, as long as it keeps the same uniformity properties. Bayesian optimization software may change in important ways between versions, and an old version cannot always be restored, for example when the tool runs as a service.

  3. Results that are easy to read

    Uniform coverage makes the results easier to reason about. A best trial at the edge of the search space is a good (but not foolproof) signal to move that boundary (Part 5). An adaptive method may neglect the middle of the space after a few unlucky early trials, even if equally good points sit there, because that kind of non-uniformity is how it speeds up the search.

  4. Parallel or sequential, same statistics

    Running trials in parallel or one after another does not change a quasi-random study statistically. For an adaptive method it does.

  5. Infeasible trials

    More sophisticated search algorithms may not handle infeasible trials (runs that diverge or fail) correctly, especially if they were not designed for neural network tuning.

  6. Simple, and strong at high parallelism

    Anecdotally, the authors find it very hard for an adaptive algorithm to beat quasi-random search given twice its budget, especially when many trials run in parallel. Without expertise in these methods, they add, you may not get the benefits the methods can in principle provide.

The preference is stronger still when one study samples the scientific hyperparameters together with the nuisance ones, because quasi-random search samples the scientific hyperparameters uniformly.

The last reason depends on how many trials run at once. An adaptive method can only learn between waves, where a wave is a batch of trials launched together. runs the same 16 trials in two ways.

The Playbook recommends Bayesian optimization after exploration and when parallelism is low.

  • After exploration. Once exploration has settled which hyperparameters matter and over what ranges, the priority shifts from understanding the problem to producing one configuration to use. Many of the advantages above then no longer apply, and the Playbook recommends Bayesian optimization tools to find the best configuration automatically, over a refined search space with as large a budget as possible (the Playbook's section on what to do after exploration ↗). It points to Open-Source Vizier. If the search space contains many divergent points, it recommends a tool that handles them properly, such as the method of Gelbart et al. . Part 6 covers this final study.
  • Low parallelism. If you can run only a few trials at a time but can afford many in sequence, the Playbook says Bayesian optimization "becomes much more attractive despite making our tuning results harder to interpret."

The authors present these advantages as their experience, and they qualify the claim about twice the budget. Their footnote credits Ben Recht and Kevin Jamieson with pointing out how strong random search with twice the budget is as a baseline, and adds that "it is certainly possible to find search spaces and problems where state-of-the-art Bayesian optimization techniques crush random search that has 2X the budget."

How many trials?

Every study returns a best trial, whether it ran 6 trials or 60. The practical question is how much that best trial depends on luck, meaning on which configurations the search happened to try. The Playbook's direct answer is that "there is no way to answer this question in general", and it offers one example instead (the Playbook's section on how many trials ↗).

A short calculation shows why a few trials leave so much to luck. Suppose the configurations good enough to win fill a fraction $p$ of the search space. Each random trial falls there with probability $p$. The chance $P_K$ that at least one of $K$ trials falls there is

$$P_K = 1 - (1 - p)^{K}.$$

With $p = 0.1$, six trials reach the good region 47% of the time, and 20 trials 88% of the time. A 95% chance takes about $3/p$ trials, 29 for the best tenth of the space and 59 for the best twentieth. The calculation assumes independent random trials.

With 20 trials and a good region that fills 10 % of the search space, the chance that at least one trial falls in it is 87.8 %. A 95% chance needs 29 trials.

Where $3/p$ comes from, and the distribution of the best of $K$

Write $\delta$ for the accepted chance of missing the region. Setting $1 - (1-p)^K \ge 1 - \delta$ and taking logarithms gives

$$K \ge \frac{\ln \delta}{\ln(1 - p)}.$$

For $\delta = 0.05$, $-\ln \delta \approx 3.0$, and for small $p$, $-\ln(1-p) \approx p$, so $K \approx 3/p$.

The same argument gives the whole distribution of the best trial. If one trial's validation error $Y$ has cumulative distribution $F$, the best of $K$ independent trials, $Y_{(1)} = \min_i Y_i$, satisfies

$$\Pr(Y_{(1)} \le y) = 1 - \bigl(1 - F(y)\bigr)^{K}.$$

Each extra trial pushes this distribution toward the lower tail of $F$ and narrows it, which is the narrowing of the boxes in . When studies are simulated by resampling a finite set of $N$ finished trials, $F$ is the empirical distribution of those trials, so no simulated study can beat the best of the $N$. That floor matters once $K$ is a sizable fraction of $N$.

The Playbook's example uses real trials. The authors tuned a ResNet-50 on ImageNet with 100 trials and then simulated smaller budgets by bootstrapping, as sketches.

The result is the Playbook's Figure 3, which you can open from the toolbar of . Its bars are easy to misread. Each box summarizes the best validation error across many simulated studies of one size. The box spans the middle half of those studies (the interquartile range, or IQR), and the line inside marks the median study. The bars therefore measure study-to-study variance, not the noise of retraining one model. The Playbook gives retraining noise separately, in its text, as about $\pm 0.1\%$ on a validation error rate of about 23%.

In the Playbook's figure the IQR is about 0.020 at 6 trials and about 0.004 at 20 (my readings of the plot). The authors point out how much larger the IQRs are at 6 trials than at 20, and they conclude that "even with 20 trials, it is likely that the difference between especially lucky and unlucky studies will be larger than the typical variation between re-trains of this model on different random seeds, with fixed hyperparameters."

These two spreads have names in the Playbook's section on comparing results (the Playbook's section on adopting a change ↗). Trial variance, also called retrain variance, is the variation between training runs that use the same hyperparameters and different random seeds. Study variance is the variation caused by the procedure that selects the hyperparameters, such as rerunning the same search with a different quasi-random seed. The Playbook notes that study variance depends on the number of trials and on the search space, and that the authors have seen it both larger and much smaller than trial variance. In practice

Each step's tag gives its source. Checking the budget by resampling is my suggestion.

  1. Explore with quasi-random search

    Open-Source Vizier has an implementation (set algorithm="QUASI_RANDOM_SEARCH"), and so does the MLCommons Halton generator. Both generate Halton sequences.

    Playbook

  2. Fall back to random search, and to a grid only in one or two dimensions

    Uniform random search is likely to be slightly less efficient than quasi-random search. A grid is acceptable for one or two hyperparameters and wasteful beyond that.

    Playbook

  3. Give the learning rate the first coordinate

    Bousquet et al. recommend assigning the smallest Halton bases to the hyperparameters that matter most, since base 2 fills its axis evenly from the first few trials.

    Bousquet et al. (2017)

  4. Check the budget by resampling your own trials

    Bootstrap smaller studies from the trials you have, as the Playbook did for its Figure 3, and compare the spread of their best with the retrain spread of your best trial. If the study-to-study spread is larger than the differences you want to detect, your conclusions depend on sampling variability. This check can say little about budgets close to the number of trials you ran.

    This post

  5. Switch to Bayesian optimization for the final study, or when parallelism is low

    Once exploration is over, run the final study with Bayesian optimization over the refined search space, using a tool that handles divergent trials. Bayesian optimization also becomes attractive earlier if you can run only a few trials at a time.

    Playbook
