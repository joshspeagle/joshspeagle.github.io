Where to put your trials, and how many to run

Josh Speagle Notes on the Playbook's section on quasi-random search ↗

Part of a series of notes on Google Research's Deep Learning Tuning Playbook. Read what the Playbook is and how these notes work.

Once a study's hyperparameters and ranges are fixed, two questions remain: where inside those bounds should each trial go, and how many trials should you run? A regular grid spends small budgets poorly, so the Playbook advises quasi-random search while you are still learning the space. On trial count, the authors state bluntly that no universal rule decides how many trials suffice. Below I unpack why grids fail by projection, how low-discrepancy sequences spread coverage, why Bayesian optimization waits until late exploration or low parallelism, and how bootstrapped ResNet-50 studies quantify trial-count luck.

In brief
  • Skip grids past two dimensions: Grids waste trials repeating weak coordinates; random or quasi-random layouts test a distinct learning rate in every run (What a grid tests).
  • Use scrambled low-discrepancy sequences for exploration: Shifted Halton or scrambled Sobol points shrink empty gaps along top parameters (Spreading trials evenly).
  • Save Bayesian optimization for exploitation or sequential runs: High parallelism blunts adaptive waves, though BoTorch, Optuna, and ASHA shine once exploration converges (Why not Bayesian optimization).
  • Measure budget stability against retrain noise: Roughly $3/p$ trials catch a top-$p$ valley at 95%; compare bootstrapped IQR against retrain spread (How many trials).

What a grid tests

Every study samples $K$ trials across a search space, requiring a placement rule upfront. Tuning learning rate and weight decay naturally suggests a grid testing every coordinate pair. A grid feels thorough because no combination is skipped.

Hyperparameters rarely carry equal weight, however. Bergstra and Bengio [3] showed that on most datasets only a handful of knobs dominate validation error, and which ones matter shifts by task. Suppose validation error responds mainly to learning rate, and compare a 9-trial grid against uniform random search in Figure 1.

Each column of the grid varies only weight decay, buying nine runs to inspect just three learning rates. Random search samples every coordinate uniformly and independently, probing nine distinct learning rates at the same compute cost.

That waste compounds quickly with dimension. A full grid of $k$ levels in $d$ dimensions consumes $k^d$ points, leaving $k = \lfloor K^{1/d} \rfloor$ values per axis: sixteen trials over four parameters sample only two distinct learning rates. For that reason the Playbook permits grids in one or two dimensions and rejects them above two, echoing Bergstra and Bengio (implementations ↗).

Spreading trials evenly with quasi-random search

Random search eliminates coordinate ties, yet independent draws still clump, crowding some intervals while leaving wide gaps untested. A direct metric for coverage is the largest gap, the widest empty stretch along an axis. I find it the sharpest check on a sample, because an unseen optimum can hide anywhere inside that unprobed interval.

The random draw in Figure 2 leaves a gap roughly three times even spacing, matching a typical seed: 0.19 equals the median over 1,000 random 16-trial draws (0.19).

The remedy is random search's distinct coordinates without its clumping: quasi-random search follows a low-discrepancy sequence so every prefix stays evenly distributed. The Playbook describes it as "jittered, shuffled grid search" that "uniformly, but randomly, explores a given search space and spreads out the search points more than random search" (quasi-random search ↗).

The generators linked in the Playbook build Halton sequences, whose construction fits in a few lines. Indexing trials $i \ge 1$ in base $b$ reflects digits across the radix point to map $1, 2, 3, \dots$ onto interior fractions $1/2, 1/4, 3/4, \dots$, splitting the widest open interval on each draw while using distinct prime bases (3, 5, 7, ...) across remaining axes. Following Bousquet et al. [4], production implementations scramble the digits and apply a random modular shift so different study seeds visit different locations.

To compare all three layouts side by side, Figure 3 places up to 100 trials in two to four dimensions while panel (d) tracks each rule's largest learning-rate gap.

Two patterns in Figure 3 jump out. In four dimensions a grid's largest gap freezes at 0.5 from 16 trials all the way to 80, because the next full grid jumps to $3^4 = 81$. Meanwhile the quasi-random gap drops sharply past each power of two while the random gap narrows noisily.

Wait: do not oversell the gap advantage. It is real yet modest, and shrinks as dimension grows: Bergstra and Bengio showed low-discrepancy sampling excels in low dimensions (roughly one to five) yet offers diminishing gains over random search when many parameters crowd a fixed budget [3]. Likewise, in higher dimensions Halton sequences suffer pairwise correlations across large prime bases, so modern numerical libraries default to base-2 scrambled Sobol sequences [9][7] whenever many axes vary together. Still, if no low-discrepancy generator sits nearby, plain random search remains a solid baseline, only "likely to be slightly less efficient" per the Playbook.

The expected largest gap of random search

Drop $K$ independent uniform points on $[0, 1]$. They cut the interval into $K + 1$ spacings (including the two boundary pieces), whose largest piece $\Delta_{\max}$ has mean

$$\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{\max}] = \frac{H_{K+1}}{K+1},$$

where $H_n = 1 + \tfrac12 + \dots + \tfrac1n$. Because spacings of uniform draws match $n = K + 1$ independent unit exponentials divided by their sum, and the maximum of $n$ unit exponentials has expectation $H_n$ independent of the normalized vector's sum, the expectation factorizes cleanly.

Even spacing yields $1/(K+1)$, making random search's average worst gap $H_{K+1}$ times wider. Since $H_n \approx \ln n + \gamma_{\text{EM}}$ ($\gamma_{\text{EM}} \approx 0.577$),

$$\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{\max}] \approx \frac{\ln(K+1) + 0.577}{K+1}.$$

At $K = 16$, $H_{17} \approx 3.4$ predicts an expected gap near 0.20, close to the simulated median 0.19 (right skew lifts the mean slightly above median). The logarithmic factor makes random search fall gradually further behind low-discrepancy sequences as $K$ increases.

How the Halton sequence spreads its points

For trial index $i \ge 1$, expand $i$ in prime base $b$ as $i = \sum_j a_j b^j$ (starting at $i = 1$ keeps every unshifted point inside the open unit cube $(0, 1)^d$). The radical-inverse map reflects those digits past the radix point,

$$\phi_b(i) = \sum_j a_j\, b^{-j-1},$$

and places the $i$-th point at $(\phi_2(i), \phi_3(i), \phi_5(i), \dots)$. In base 2, indices $1, \dots, 2^m - 1$ fill all $2^m - 1$ strict interior multiples $\{k/2^m : 1 \le k < 2^m\}$, slicing $[0, 1)$ into even $2^{-m}$ gaps after every power-of-two threshold and generating the stepped drops in panel (d) of Figure 3.

Bousquet et al. [4] permute nonzero digits and add a random shift mod 1 so different seeds sample distinct points without losing uniformity. While Hammersley sets need $K$ fixed upfront, Halton adds trials online without relocating earlier runs. Bousquet et al. also prove that a grid's projected spacing shrinks as $K^{-1/d}$ in $d$ dimensions, whereas Halton and random search scale with the number of active parameters, matching Figure 1. Give base 2 and small primes to the most sensitive hyperparameters, because base $b$ fills its interval only once $K > b$. Modern Sobol generators combine Joe–Kuo direction numbers [7] with Owen digit scrambling [9] to keep those projections crisp even past a dozen dimensions.

Why not Bayesian optimization?

Every sequence above is non-adaptive: trial coordinates are fixed before any training job finishes. An adaptive search feeds completed results into later choices, fitting surrogate models over performance [10] in Bayesian optimization or mutating top trials in evolution. Using feedback sounds strictly stronger, so I was initially surprised the Playbook delays adaptive search during exploration.

The choice hinges on the goal of a round. In exploration you seek structural insight rather than one peak point, giving quasi-random search six decisive advantages (quasi-random search ↗).

  1. Change the objective afterward

    Since trial placement ignores scores, you can re-rank the finished study on final loss, calibration, or training loss without rerunning a job.

  2. Reproducible studies

    Old studies reproduce cleanly without fragile optimizer state, whereas complex Bayesian surrogates drift across library updates or hosted services.

  3. Results that are easy to read

    Uniform coverage makes boundary checks trustworthy (Part 5); adaptive sampling intentionally abandons weak early regions, skewing axis plots.

  4. Parallel or sequential, same statistics

    Running trials in one large parallel wave or serially produces identical sampling statistics, whereas adaptive batching changes every subsequent draw.

  5. Infeasible trials

    Diverged or NaN runs (Part 5) leave quasi-random placement untouched, whereas naive Bayesian surrogates break unless explicitly constrained.

  6. Simple, and strong at high parallelism

    The authors note adaptive tools struggle to beat quasi-random search given twice the budget in parallel settings, and demand expertise to tune well.

When a study varies scientific hyperparameters alongside nuisance settings, uniform placement also gives every scientific candidate equal nuisance coverage.

Parallelism explains why wave geometry matters. Adaptive methods only learn between batches of completed jobs, so Figure 4 contrasts running 16 trials in one wave versus four.

None of this sidelines adaptive search altogether. The Playbook recommends Bayesian optimization once exploration finishes, and whenever parallelism is low:

  • After exploration. Once exploration narrows which hyperparameters matter and bounds their ranges, priorities flip from understanding to squeezing out peak validation performance. Run automated search over that tightened space using Open-Source Vizier (plus constrained divergence handling [5]), or open toolkits such as Optuna [1] and BoTorch [2] (whose batched Monte-Carlo acquisition functions and asynchronous evaluation handle parallel clusters far better than classic serial Gaussian processes). Part 6 covers this final study.
  • Low parallelism and asynchronous multi-fidelity pruning. Running few workers serially makes Bayesian optimization attractive "despite making our tuning results harder to interpret." For pure late-stage optimization with noisy early curves, asynchronous successive halving (ASHA) [8] reclaims compute by terminating weak trials early without waiting on synchronous barriers. Keep that speed separate from scientific exploration: pruning short-circuits slow-starting runs and violates the Playbook's full-budget checkpoint rule (Part 1).

Keep these trade-offs calibrated as practical guidance rather than theorems. The Playbook footnote credits Ben Recht and Kevin Jamieson with showing random search at double budget is a tough baseline, while adding that "it is certainly possible to find search spaces and problems where state-of-the-art Bayesian optimization techniques crush random search that has 2X the budget."

How many trials are enough?

With placement settled, how many trials should a study run? Every search returns a best trial whether given 6 runs or 60, making luck in the sampled coordinates critical. The Playbook is direct that "there is no way to answer this question in general", offering one empirical check instead (trial counts ↗).

Even without a general theorem, elementary probability shows why small budgets wobble. Suppose winning settings cover a fraction $p$ of the search volume so each independent draw hits with probability $p$. At least one of $K$ draws falls inside that good region with probability

$$P_K = 1 - (1 - p)^{K}.$$

At $p = 0.1$, six trials hit the good region just 47% of the time and 20 trials reach 88%. Clearing 95% needs $\lceil \ln(0.05)/\ln(1-p) \rceil$, or 29 trials at $p = 0.1$ and 59 at $p = 0.05$ (close to the quick $3/p$ rule of thumb of 30 and 60). Independent draws only bound quasi-random performance roughly, yet the scaling holds: halving the sweet spot doubles the required budget.

With 20 trials and a good region filling 10 % of the search space, at least one trial hits it with probability 87.8 %. A 95% guarantee takes 29 trials.

Where $3/p$ comes from, and the distribution of the best of $K$

Let $\delta$ be the acceptable failure risk. Solving $1 - (1-p)^K \ge 1 - \delta$ yields

$$K \ge \frac{\ln \delta}{\ln(1 - p)}.$$

For $\delta = 0.05$, $-\ln \delta \approx 3.0$, and for small $p$, $-\ln(1-p) \approx p$, yielding the approximation $K \approx 3/p$ (just above the exact integer counts 29 at $p=0.1$ and 59 at $p=0.05$).

Order statistics generalize that tail calculation. If a single trial's error $Y$ follows cumulative distribution $F$, the best of $K$ independent draws, $Y_{(1)} = \min_i Y_i$, follows

$$\Pr(Y_{(1)} \le y) = 1 - \bigl(1 - F(y)\bigr)^{K}.$$

Adding trials shifts the minimum toward the lower tail of $F$ and shrinks its spread, matching the narrowing boxes in Figure 6. When resampling a finite pool of $N$ finished runs, no bootstrap can beat the best run in the pool, an artificial floor that bites once $K$ approaches $N$.

The Playbook grounds this variance in an ImageNet ResNet-50 experiment: the authors ran 100 trials and bootstrapped smaller study budgets, as sketched in Figure 5.

That produces the Playbook's Figure 3, openable from the toolbar of Figure 6. Its boxes are easy to misread at first: each box plots the middle half (interquartile range, or IQR) and median of the winning validation score across many resampled studies of size $K$. The spread measures study-to-study luck rather than retrain seed noise, which the Playbook quotes separately as about $\pm 0.1\%$ around a 23% ImageNet error rate.

Reading the plot, I measure an IQR near 0.020 at 6 trials and 0.004 at 20. Highlighting that fivefold drop, the authors write that "even with 20 trials, it is likely that the difference between especially lucky and unlucky studies will be larger than the typical variation between re-trains of this model on different random seeds, with fixed hyperparameters."

In its adopting-a-change section ↗, the Playbook separates these two noises cleanly. Trial variance (retrain variance) reflects seed-to-seed variation at fixed hyperparameters; study variance comes from resampled hyperparameter draws. Depending on study budget and space design, study variance can dwarf or fall well below trial variance (Part 6 weighs both before launching changes).

To compare those spreads directly, I rebuilt the experiment as an interactive toy in Figure 6. A pool of 100 synthetic runs replaces the ImageNet pool, calibrated so its 6- and 20-trial spreads match the Playbook. Beside each quartile box, a red bar marks the $\pm 0.1\%$ retrain band around the median, and an optional $K = 50$ toggle extends the sequence past the Playbook's 20-trial upper bound.

Just like the Playbook's plot, a 20-trial study in this toy still varies about twice as much as retraining one model. Two 20-trial seeds can separate by 0.004 purely from trial placement, a spread to keep in mind before calling a candidate winner in Part 6.

In practice

Use these steps when setting trial placement and budget; tags indicate source, with the bootstrap stability check added beyond the Playbook.

  1. Explore with quasi-random search

    Use Open-Source Vizier (algorithm="QUASI_RANDOM_SEARCH") or the MLCommons Halton generator during exploration; switch to scrambled Sobol sequences [9][7] when tuning many parameters at once. Playbook / QMC

  2. Fall back to random search, and to a grid only in one or two dimensions

    Prefer uniform random sampling over multidimensional grids, reserving grids strictly for one- or two-parameter studies. Playbook

  3. Give the learning rate the first coordinate

    Assign base 2 (the smallest Halton prime) to learning rate and the next smallest bases to your highest-impact parameters so early trials bisect their axes cleanly [4]. Bousquet et al. (2017)

  4. Check the budget by resampling finished trials

    Bootstrap smaller study sizes from your completed runs and compare their winning-score IQR against your retrain noise band. If study spread exceeds the margin you care about, increase trial budget or shrink the search space (remembering resamples saturate near the full pool size). This post

  5. Switch to Bayesian optimization after exploration, or at low parallelism

    Once exploration settles the search box, hand exploitation to Bayesian optimization (Vizier, Optuna, or BoTorch with divergence handling) or use ASHA only when pure wall-clock optimization outweighs full-curve diagnostics. Playbook / HPO

Where this fits

Search placement and trial count sit inside designing every study in the loop. Once those runs finish, check them carefully for boundary clipping and bad curves before believing the winner (Part 5).
