Input pipelines, evaluation, checkpoints and multi-host training

Josh Speagle Notes on the Playbook's section on the training pipeline ↗

Part of a series of notes on Google Research's Deep Learning Tuning Playbook. Read what the Playbook is and how these notes work.

Every earlier post assumed that your training pipeline computes clean losses, measures comparable steps, and keeps accelerators fed. The Playbook closes its main guide with operational rules that protect those assumptions against silent bugs that corrupt numbers without crashing jobs. I open this final post with preflight smoke tests of my own, then trace Playbook advice across input profiling and modern compilation, evaluation timing and checkpoint buffers, study tracking, normalization across devices, and sharded multi-host runs.

In brief
  • Run smoke checks first: Inspect decoded tensors, verify step-0 loss near $\ln C$, and overfit one tiny batch before scaling runs (Smoke tests).
  • Keep accelerators fed without hidden stalls: Profile host I/O, isolate compile warmup, and strip accidental host sync barriers (Input pipelines).
  • Evaluate on step intervals and log buffers: Step clocks align runs cleanly; sample sizes and rare-class counts govern statistical sensitivity (Evaluating during training).
  • Replace batch norm and guard sharded state: Prefer RMSNorm or LayerNorm where possible, and align seeds, shards, and async checkpoints across hosts (Multi-host pipelines).

Smoke tests before the first sweep

Author Prelude This preflight section is my addition before the Playbook's seven pipeline sections begin.

Whereas the Playbook opens with throughput tuning, I ask first whether the loss, targets, and gradients are wired properly. Speed never rescues a miswired loss because fast broken runs just waste sweep compute faster. Three zero-tuning checks (Figure 1), adapted from Andrej Karpathy's A Recipe for Training Neural Networks [8], catch silent pipeline bugs before your first tuning job.

Notice that none of these checks measures speed. A pipeline can pass all three and still leave the accelerator idle for half of every step, and that failure is where the Playbook's own list begins.

Where does the accelerator wait?

Suppose a training step takes 100 ms of accelerator time, yet the job runs at about four steps per second instead of ten. Where did the rest of the time go? Usually it goes into reading files, decoding and augmenting examples, and copying the next batch to the device. A pipeline in this state is input-bound, meaning its speed is set by how fast the host produces examples while the accelerator idles in between. The Playbook is candid that there is no universal fix.

The Playbook Optimizing the input pipeline ↗

The causes and interventions of input-bound pipelines are highly task-dependent; use a profiler and look out for common issues.

A profiler answers the question directly by recording a timeline of what the host and the accelerator are each doing. The Playbook points to Perfetto for JAX and to the TensorFlow profiler, and PyTorch users have torch.profiler. To build intuition for what such a trace shows, I made the simplified timeline in Figure 2, where one input worker on the host reads and preprocesses each batch and the accelerator then runs the training step on it.

Every input stall reduces to one race: host batch preparation versus device step compute (Figure 2). The Playbook flags three common causes:

  • Data far from the training process. Reading training data over a network adds I/O latency to every batch. In the toy this is the network-storage setting, where the read segment dominates.
  • Expensive online preprocessing. Decoding and augmenting on the fly can cost more than the training step itself, so the Playbook suggests doing such preprocessing once, offline, and saving the result.
  • Unintentional synchronization barriers. Calling the host back from device execution stalls prefetching, as when logging device metrics synchronously through CommonLoopUtils (or calling scalar synchronization such as .item() inside an inner loop).

The remedies mirror that diagnosis. Prefetching (tf.data.Dataset.prefetch, or PyTorch DataLoader workers with num_workers > 0, pin_memory=True, and persistent_workers=True) hides data loading behind device math. Stripping unused metadata early cuts transfer cost, and adding parallel input workers raises arrival throughput. Still, engineering constraints such as storage limits can justify accepting slower I/O.

Before opening a heavy profiler, I run one quick ablation: feed the step loop a single tensor batch already sitting in device memory and compare steps per second against your real input loader. Any wide throughput gap confirms an input stall, echoing Part 3's doubling test.

In modern compiled frameworks, separate compilation startup from steady-state throughput. Using PyTorch 2's torch.compile (built around TorchDynamo and TorchInductor [1]), skip early warm-up steps when timing batch speed. Keep input shapes padded symmetrically as well, because shifting batch sizes during partial evaluation batches or batch-doubling sweeps triggers dynamic-shape recompiles that look like data stalls.

Evaluating during training

Once batches stream smoothly, verify what the loop measures. Figure 3 separates three evaluation settings from the Playbook. Online evaluation tracks live production predictions, offline evaluation scores full held-out datasets mimicking production, and periodic evaluation computes proxy validation metrics at intervals during training. Hyperparameter tuning lives almost wholly in periodic evaluation, where timing and batching traps arise.

Periodic evaluations serve three purposes: tracking live runs, feeding retrospective checkpoint selection, and drawing training curves after completion. When training and evaluation alternate on the same device, the Playbook advises setting evaluation batch size at least as large as training batch size since evaluation discards activations rather than storing a backward pass.

Step intervals, not time intervals

The next decision is when to evaluate. A timer is the easiest schedule to write ("evaluate every ten minutes"), and it has the appeal of making the cost of evaluation predictable. The Playbook nonetheless argues against it.

The Playbook Setting up periodic evaluations ↗

Periodic evaluations should be done at regular step intervals, not time intervals. Evaluating based on time intervals can make it harder to interpret the training curves, especially when training may suffer from preemptions of the training jobs, network latency issues, etc.

To see why the choice matters, I built Figure 4, which replays one simulated run under both schedules. The job trains at 10 steps per second, slows to 3 steps per second for 600 s of network contention, and is later preempted and restarted from its last checkpoint.

Timers space evaluations evenly across seconds rather than steps. Slowdowns crowd evaluations over three times tighter on the step axis, while preemptions evaluate repeated steps at misaligned points. Neither artifact measures the network; both merely reflect cluster jitter.

Worse, uneven spacing skews checkpoint selection. Because the best checkpoint takes a minimum over noisy validation scores, crowded regions buy extra lottery draws. Here the timer picks step 10,075 inside the slowdown window, whereas the step clock picks 6,500 near the true minimum. Fixed step schedules align every trial at identical evaluation steps so every model receives equal footing.

Periodic patterns in validation metrics

Step intervals bring a second bonus the Playbook flags. When data splits should be shuffled, regular waves across periodic metrics expose dataset leakage or broken shuffling immediately. Though the Playbook skips the explanation, fixed epochs span fixed step counts, keeping pass-aligned bugs locked at constant period where timers smear them across irregular wall intervals.

Partial batches and saved predictions

Small boundary errors bias metrics too. When evaluation counts do not divide evenly by batch size, pipelines pad the trailing partial batch. Weight padded slots cleanly (zero weight is standard, Figure 5) so dummy tensors never pollute validation means.

Inside every evaluation pass, the Playbook recommends logging individual model predictions alongside aggregate scores. Saving inspectable model artifacts such as TensorFlow SavedModels enables rapid post-hoc error analysis after jobs conclude.

Choosing the evaluation sample

Alongside when to score, decide what slice to score. Because running periodic passes over a huge offline test suite stalls training, pipelines routinely define a compact periodic evaluation sample balancing four survey criteria:

  • No skew. Performance on the sample should match performance on the whole offline evaluation set.
  • Size. The sample should be small enough to evaluate in full without trouble, yet large enough to measure improvements to the model accurately, so that they are not overwhelmed by label noise.
  • Repeated use. It should be large enough to support many evaluations across trials in sequence without adaptively fitting the validation set in a way that fails to generalize to a held-out test set, although the Playbook adds that this is rarely a practical concern. (For the theory of reusing a holdout set, see Dwork et al. (2015) [3].)
  • Imbalanced classes. Performance on rare classes will often be noisy, so for a class with few examples the Playbook suggests logging the number of examples predicted correctly.

The authors illustrate the last point by asking, ".05 sensitivity improvement sounds exciting, but was it just one more example correct?" With 20 validation examples in a class, the answer is yes (Figure 6).

How large a sample resolves a given improvement

The Playbook leaves "large enough" qualitative, so here is a rough guide of my own based on the binomial distribution. An accuracy $p$ measured on $n$ independent examples has standard error

$$\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{p\,(1-p)}{n}}.$$

For $p = 0.9$, the standard error is about 0.009 on 1,000 examples and 0.003 on 10,000, and a difference between two checkpoints smaller than about twice the standard error is hard to separate from sampling noise. Comparing two models on the same examples is less noisy than this suggests, because the examples that both get right or both get wrong cancel out of the difference. For a rare class, $n$ is the number of examples in that class, which is why its metrics move in steps of $1/n$.

Checkpoints and retrospective selection

The Playbook Saving checkpoints ↗

Run training for a fixed number of steps and retrospectively choose the best checkpoint from the run.

Most frameworks already save the model's state to disk periodically, mainly so that a training job survives interruptions. The Playbook's point is that those same checkpoints can do more work, because the best one is often not the last, particularly once validation performance stops improving and fluctuates about a value. Its recommendation is for the pipeline to keep the N best checkpoints seen so far (a checkpoint buffer) and choose the best of them at the end of training, a procedure it calls retrospective optimal checkpoint selection. Since each trial runs for a pre-specified number of steps and the best checkpoints are kept along the way, prospective early stopping (ending a run when validation performance stalls) is usually unnecessary.

Retrospective selection revisits Figure 4's count bias directly. Scoring a run by its minimum noisy metric pulls the score downward as evaluation count $M$ rises (Part 4). You cannot eliminate noise completely, yet evaluating at fixed steps keeps $M$ equal across compared trials so bias cancels in pairwise comparisons.

How much lower does the minimum of more evaluations fall?

This calculation is my addition rather than the Playbook's. If a stretch of training has a level validation error $\bar V$ and each evaluation adds independent noise with standard deviation $\sigma$, the minimum of $M$ evaluations falls below $\bar V$ by an amount that grows with $M$. For large $M$ the expected shortfall approaches $\sigma\sqrt{2\ln M}$, and for $M$ between 5 and 25 it is about $1.2\sigma$ to $2.0\sigma$ (the factors $m_K$ in the selection-bias aside of Part 6). In Figure 4 the slowdown holds 24 timer evaluations against 8 step-scheduled ones, enough to move the selected checkpoint.

Experiment tracking

The Playbook Setting up experiment tracking ↗

When tracking different experiments, make sure to note a number of essentials like the best performance of a checkpoint in the study, and a short description of the study.

Everything so far concerns a single trial, but over weeks of tuning the results that matter end up spread across dozens of studies, and a careful evaluation is of little use if nobody can find it later. The Playbook's authors keep their experiment tracking results in a spreadsheet with the six columns in Figure 8. They are not attached to the tool, asking only for a system that captures at least this information and is convenient for the people using it, and they close with a blunt warning: "Untracked experiments might as well not exist."

Hosted experiment trackers record configurations and curves automatically, which takes care of the config, trial and performance columns. The description and reproduction columns are different, because someone still has to write them while the study is fresh, and a returning reader has no other way to recover what they record.

Batch norm across devices

The Playbook Batch normalization implementation details ↗

Nowadays batch norm can often be replaced with LayerNorm, but in cases where it cannot, there are tricky details when changing the batch size or number of hosts.

Like the multi-host issues in the next section, these details produce bugs that are hard to spot, because the code runs and the numbers look plausible. Recall that batch norm [7] normalizes each feature (a channel, in a convolutional network) using the mean and variance of that feature over a group of $n$ examples. For an activation $z_i$ of example $i$,

$$\hat{z}_i = \frac{z_i - \htmlClass{math-col-model}{\bar{z}}}{\sqrt{\htmlClass{math-col-model}{s^2} + \epsilon}}, \qquad \htmlClass{math-col-model}{\bar{z}} = \frac{1}{\htmlClass{math-col-third}{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{\htmlClass{math-col-third}{n}} z_j,$$

where $s^2$ is the variance over the same $n$ examples and $\epsilon$ is a small constant. At test time the layer instead uses exponential moving averages (EMAs) of these statistics collected during training. The subtlety the Playbook raises first is that with several devices, each holding part of the batch, the statistics differ from device to device unless they are explicitly synchronized. Without synchronization, $n$ equals the per-device batch size, $B/D$ for a global batch size $B$ split over $D$ devices, which means that changing either the batch size or the device count also changes $n$.

Why does $n$ matter mathematically? Changing group count alters the loss objective itself. Goyal et al. (2017) [5] warn: "if the per-worker minibatch sample size $n$ is changed, it changes the underlying loss function $L$ that is optimized", holding $n = 32$ fixed across large-batch ImageNet experiments. Figure 9 maps what four normalizers average over.

Which group size $n$ works well? Anecdotally (primarily on ImageNet), the Playbook reports good empirical behavior near 64 examples per batch-norm estimate, citing ghost batch norm [6]. Keep numbers separate: 64 is the Playbook authors' anecdote, whereas Hoffer et al. trained CIFAR with virtual batches of 128. Either choice decouples statistics from global batch size (Part 3).

Two implementation details remain critical for clean evaluation:

  • Per-device batches larger than the ghost batch. Ghost batch norm implementations do not always handle a per-device batch size larger than the virtual batch size. In that case each device has to subsample its batch to compute statistics over the intended number of examples.
  • The EMAs used at test time. Moving averages combine statistics linearly, so synchronizing them across devices right before checkpointing ensures test consistency. Common wrappers otherwise silently dump host-0's unaveraged EMA alone.

Modern transformer pipelines sidestep this book-keeping entirely by dropping batch norm. LayerNorm [2], RMSNorm [10] (which drops mean-centering while preserving scaling invariance), and GroupNorm [9] compute activations strictly within each example, eliminating cross-device synchronization bugs whenever batch size or node counts scale.

Multi-host pipelines

The Playbook Considerations for multi-host pipelines ↗

For logging, evals, RNGs, checkpointing, and data sharding, multi-host training can make it very easy to introduce bugs!

A multi-host pipeline runs one training job across several machines, each driving its own accelerators. In the usual setup every host runs the same program, and that symmetry is the source of the trouble, since anything that should happen only once or differ between hosts has to be arranged explicitly. I find it easiest to read the Playbook's four rules as a statement of what should be shared across hosts and what should differ, which is how Figure 10 lays them out.

  • Log and checkpoint on one host. Otherwise every host writes the same files, and the copies overwrite or duplicate one another.
  • Synchronize batch norm statistics before evaluation or checkpointing. The saved model then reflects the statistics of all hosts, which is the multi-host form of the EMA point in the batch norm section.
  • Mark each RNG seed by its role. Use identical seeds across hosts for weight initialization, yet distinct per-host seeds for shuffling and augmentation. Otherwise replicas diverge from different starting points or feed duplicate batches from shared files.
  • Shard data files across hosts. The Playbook recommends this for performance.

Two modern distributed realities extend those 2023 rules:

  • Sharded states and async checkpoints. Under Fully Sharded Data Parallelism (PyTorch FSDP [11]), weights, gradients, and optimizer moments stay partitioned across ranks and materialize one unit at a time during computation. Gathering everything onto host 0 hits host-RAM limits, so production pipelines save distributed checkpoints asynchronously in parallel across hosts (for example via torch.distributed.checkpoint or Orbax async).
  • Mixed-precision and TensorCore non-determinism. Even with locked initialization and shuffle seeds, fast parallel kernels and bf16 all-reduce reductions vary subtly in floating-point accumulation order across runs, reinforcing the seed-rerun variance checks from Part 6.

In practice

Work through this operational checklist by pipeline stage before tuning, inside each training job, and after every study completes.

  1. Pass three preflight smoke tests Author addition

    Decode and inspect one final batch, confirm initial loss sits near $\ln C$ for classification, and overfit one small batch before launching your first sweep (Smoke tests).

  2. Benchmark input throughput and warm up compiled graphs

    Compare live training throughput against a cached in-memory batch, prefetch across input workers, drop accidental sync calls, and keep padded batch shapes static past initial torch.compile warmup (Where the accelerator waits).

  3. Align distributed hosts, seeds, shards, and async saves

    Unify initialization seeds, isolate per-host data seeds and file shards, log scalars once, and save sharded model/optimizer states asynchronously without saturating host 0 RAM (Multi-host pipelines).

  4. Evaluate at regular steps with zero-padded tail weights

    Schedule validation strictly by step interval using batches at least as big as training, mask padded final-batch examples to weight zero, and save sample predictions for inspection (Evaluating during training).

  5. Keep the N best checkpoints and replace legacy BatchNorm

    Record top checkpoints continuously over fixed steps rather than stopping early; use RMSNorm, LayerNorm, or GroupNorm where possible, or sync EMA/stats before saving BatchNorm checkpoints (Normalization).

  6. Audit rare-class counts and log every study

    Report raw hit counts on small validation slices, verify periodic subset match against full offline data, and store study names, config commits, trial counts, and reproduction notes (Experiment tracking). Collect all 64 Playbook rules in Reference.
